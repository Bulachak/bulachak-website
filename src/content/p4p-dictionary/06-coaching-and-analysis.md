## Video Analysis
short: Reviewing bouts on video, touch by touch, to understand a fencer or prepare for an opponent.
avoid: scouting; scout
source: P4P house style
status: rule

Fencing does not use the word scouting. Say video analysis, bout analysis, opponent analysis, opponent preparation, match archive or video review. Analysis works through a [[Bout]] one [[Touch]] at a time.

> Can you scout our next opponent for me?
> I will do the opponent analysis tonight. Their last three matches are on video.

## Bout Notation
short: Writing down every action of a bout in agreed symbols, usually from video, so that it can be counted and compared.
avoid: bout record (when only the score is kept)
source: L. G. Ryzhkova and V. V. Shamis, Pokazateli primeneniya atak i protivodeystviy im v sorevnovatelnoy deyatelnosti uchastnits Olimpiady-2020, fekhtuyushchikh na sablyakh [Use of Attacks and Counters to Them by Women Sabre Fencers at the 2020 Olympics] (2023), pp. 38 to 39; G. B. Shustikov, V. G. Fedorov, A. V. Deev and E. A. Nechaeva, Pedagogicheskiy analiz sostava i rezultativnosti sredstv vedeniya fekhtovalnogo boya sablistami v protsesse sorevnovatelnykh turnirov [Composition and Effectiveness of Sabre Fencers' Means of Conducting a Bout across a Tournament] (2018), p. 295; G. B. Shustikov and V. G. Fedorov, Pedagogicheskiy analiz individualnykh stiley vedeniya fekhtovalnogo boya rapiristov [Individual Bout Styles of Foil Fencers] (2023), p. 474
status: library

Russian studies call it notatsionnaya zapis. Ryzhkova and Shamis watched video of the women placed first to eighth in [[Sabre]] at the Tokyo Olympics and decoded every exchange with notation and graphic symbols. They note that the method does not disturb the competition, follows both fencers at once and allows replays. Shustikov and colleagues filmed 152 sabre bouts at 6 tournaments in 2016 and 2017 and wrote them up while playing the video in slow motion, to catch the detail of each action. Shustikov and Fedorov used the same notation for 91 [[Foil]] bouts. The notation is the raw material for [[Volume of Actions]] and [[Effectiveness of Actions]]. See [[Video Analysis]].

## Volume of Actions
short: How large a share of all the actions in a set of bouts one kind of action makes up.
source: G. B. Shustikov, V. G. Fedorov, A. V. Deev and E. A. Nechaeva, Pedagogicheskiy analiz sostava i rezultativnosti sredstv vedeniya fekhtovalnogo boya sablistami v protsesse sorevnovatelnykh turnirov [Composition and Effectiveness of Sabre Fencers' Means of Conducting a Bout across a Tournament] (2018), p. 295; L. G. Ryzhkova and V. V. Shamis, Pokazateli primeneniya atak i protivodeystviy im v sorevnovatelnoy deyatelnosti uchastnits Olimpiady-2020, fekhtuyushchikh na sablyakh [Use of Attacks and Counters to Them by Women Sabre Fencers at the 2020 Olympics] (2023), p. 39
status: library

Russian bout analysis calls it obyom. Shustikov and colleagues give the formula: the number of actions of one kind, divided by the total number of actions, times 100. Volume shows what a fencer chooses to do, not whether it works; that is measured by [[Effectiveness of Actions]]. Ryzhkova and Shamis found that attacks made up 69.19 percent of all actions of the eight best women in Tokyo, and counters to the attack about a quarter (24.97 percent). They read this as the mark of [[Sabre]]: short, fast exchanges aimed at the [[Attack]], less typical of foil and épée. Volumes come from [[Bout Notation]].

## Effectiveness of Actions
short: How often a kind of action ends in a touch for the fencer who made it.
source: G. B. Shustikov, V. G. Fedorov, A. V. Deev and E. A. Nechaeva, Pedagogicheskiy analiz sostava i rezultativnosti sredstv vedeniya fekhtovalnogo boya sablistami v protsesse sorevnovatelnykh turnirov [Composition and Effectiveness of Sabre Fencers' Means of Conducting a Bout across a Tournament] (2018), pp. 295 to 296
status: library

Russian bout analysis calls it rezultativnost. Shustikov and colleagues write it as the number of actions that scored, divided by the number of actions, times 100. In their [[Direct Elimination]] sample of sabre bouts, the attack of first intention made 23.34 percent of actions and scored 68.09 percent of the time. The [[Counter-Attack]] made 11.65 percent and scored 70.09 percent. The parry with [[Riposte]] made 9.29 percent and scored 92.45 percent, and the remise, only 4.17 percent of actions, scored every time. Read with the [[Volume of Actions]], it shows which actions a fencer uses often and which ones actually win touches.

## Attack Effectiveness
short: How often attacks, and the counters to them, score; at the Tokyo Olympics in women's sabre, fewer than half did.
source: L. G. Ryzhkova and V. V. Shamis, Pokazateli primeneniya atak i protivodeystviy im v sorevnovatelnoy deyatelnosti uchastnits Olimpiady-2020, fekhtuyushchikh na sablyakh [Use of Attacks and Counters to Them by Women Sabre Fencers at the 2020 Olympics] (2023), pp. 38 to 39
status: library

Ryzhkova and Shamis analysed the eight best women sabre fencers at the Tokyo Olympics. Attacks scored 43.22 percent of the time and counters to the attack 41.51 percent, so not even every second attempt produced a hit. The winner attacked in 72.78 percent of her actions. Another Russian fencer reached 81.36 percent, which the authors explain as her effort to catch up on the score, and her counters fell to the lowest level, 14.41 percent. Because sabre is fenced in uncertainty of distance and moment, the authors ask for training of reliable attacks and counters in unexpected situations, built on specialised visual reactions, and of [[Resistance to Interference]] from early or oncoming blade movements. See [[Attack]] and [[Counter-Attack]].

> I lost four attacks in a row. Should I stop attacking?
> No. Even the Olympic finalists missed more than half. Change the preparation, not the plan.

## Mutual Attack
short: An exchange in which both fencers attack at once, each trying to start first and arrive first.
avoid: double attack (outside sabre analysis)
source: L. G. Ryzhkova and V. V. Shamis, Pokazateli primeneniya atak i protivodeystviy im v sorevnovatelnoy deyatelnosti uchastnits Olimpiady-2020, fekhtuyushchikh na sablyakh [Use of Attacks and Counters to Them by Women Sabre Fencers at the 2020 Olympics] (2023), p. 39; G. B. Shustikov, V. G. Fedorov, A. V. Deev and E. A. Nechaeva, Pedagogicheskiy analiz sostava i rezultativnosti sredstv vedeniya fekhtovalnogo boya sablistami v protsesse sorevnovatelnykh turnirov [Composition and Effectiveness of Sabre Fencers' Means of Conducting a Bout across a Tournament] (2018), pp. 296 to 297
status: library

Russian sabre studies call it oboyudnaya ataka. The [[Referee]] decides it under the rules on [[Priority]] and the [[Simultaneous Action]]. Ryzhkova and Shamis counted 5.84 percent mutual attacks among the eight best women in Tokyo. They read them as a sign that both fencers want the initiative and try to read each other's timing, and say mutual attacks can also be used to break up the opponent's tactical plan. In the sabre sample of Shustikov and colleagues, mutual attacks made 27.08 percent of actions in [[Direct Elimination]] bouts and scored 38.12 percent of the time. They were used mostly at the end of close bouts, for example at 14:14.

## Means of Conducting a Bout
short: The full set of actions a fencer uses in bouts, described by which actions appear, how often and how well they work.
source: G. B. Shustikov, V. G. Fedorov, A. V. Deev and E. A. Nechaeva, Pedagogicheskiy analiz sostava i rezultativnosti sredstv vedeniya fekhtovalnogo boya sablistami v protsesse sorevnovatelnykh turnirov [Composition and Effectiveness of Sabre Fencers' Means of Conducting a Bout across a Tournament] (2018), pp. 294 to 297; L. G. Ryzhkova, Takticheskaya informatsiya v trenirovke fekhtovalshchika [Tactical Information in Fencer Training] (2009), pp. 69 to 70
status: library

Russian literature calls it sredstva vedeniya boya, and its composition (sostav) is the main object of bout analysis. Shustikov and colleagues sort sabre actions into mutual attacks, attacks of first intention, return attacks, the point in line, the [[Remise]], the [[Counter-Attack]], the parry with riposte and the interception. They link changes in this composition to changes in the rules, the refereeing, the equipment and the scoring apparatus. Ryzhkova bases the individualisation of a fencer's competition on the same data: the typical actions, the ratios between them and the bout models used. Each kind of action is measured by [[Volume of Actions]] and [[Effectiveness of Actions]]. See [[Competition Analysis]].

## Competition Analysis
short: Comparing how fencers fence at each stage of a tournament, from the pools to the final bouts.
source: G. B. Shustikov, V. G. Fedorov, A. V. Deev and E. A. Nechaeva, Pedagogicheskiy analiz sostava i rezultativnosti sredstv vedeniya fekhtovalnogo boya sablistami v protsesse sorevnovatelnykh turnirov [Composition and Effectiveness of Sabre Fencers' Means of Conducting a Bout across a Tournament] (2018), pp. 295 to 297
status: library

Shustikov and colleagues followed 152 sabre bouts and found that each stage is fenced differently. In the [[Pool]], bouts to 5 touches are very short, so each technical, tactical or refereeing error weighs more. Fencers mostly use simple attacks there, which the opponent reads easily. In [[Direct Elimination]] bouts to 15, phrases grow longer and return attacks and counter-attacks increase. Parries fall to a third of the pool level, but their effectiveness comes close to 100 percent. In the final bouts every kind of action scores at least half the time, and the [[Counter-Attack]] scores 80 percent. Finalists make attacks harder to read with blade actions and feints. See [[Means of Conducting a Bout]].

> Why do you want me to try a compound attack in the pool?
> The pool is where you test it. In the final you will only use what you trust.

## Tactical Information
short: Everything a fencer and coach need to know to build tactics, sorted into sections from positions and distance to criteria of tactical readiness.
source: L. G. Ryzhkova, Takticheskaya informatsiya v trenirovke fekhtovalshchika [Tactical Information in Fencer Training] (2009), pp. 67 to 70
status: library

Ryzhkova was the first to put this information in order, in a scheme for long-term training. Its sections are: positions, blade movements and the fencers' places on the [[Piste]]; the ways of making attacks and counters to them; bout models; the terms of tactics and of [[Tactical Preparation]]; priorities of each weapon set by the rules; information suited to each training stage; individualisation; personalisation against different opponents (see [[Opponent Preparation]]); correction for unusual heat, humidity, time zones or altitude; and criteria for judging tactical readiness. Before every exchange the fencer checks the distance before and during it, the chosen blade movements and the moves of both fencers. She notes that a bout may need 9 to 29 touches, and a tournament 5 to 20 bouts or more.

## Opponent Preparation
short: Building a bout plan for a particular opponent, rather than fencing everyone the same way.
avoid: scouting; scout
source: L. G. Ryzhkova, Takticheskaya informatsiya v trenirovke fekhtovalshchika [Tactical Information in Fencer Training] (2009), p. 70; G. B. Shustikov and V. G. Fedorov, Pedagogicheskiy analiz individualnykh stiley vedeniya fekhtovalnogo boya rapiristov [Individual Bout Styles of Foil Fencers] (2023), p. 476
status: library

Ryzhkova calls it the personalisation of tactics against different opponents. Because one blade movement can serve many tactical intentions, and different intentions can fit the same situation, she advises leaving the standard patterns behind and looking for the best way to fence a given opponent. Its aim is to use more of the level a fencer has already reached. Shustikov and Fedorov saw the same thing in top foil fencers: against one opponent a fencer seems to repeat the same actions, while against another the set of actions grows a lot and goes beyond their usual style. The material comes from [[Video Analysis]] of the opponent's bouts and from [[Reconnaissance]] during the bout.

> She always parries four. What do I do?
> We checked her last bouts on video. Show her the four, then finish in the other line.

## Model Characteristics
short: The measured qualities that the most successful fencers in a weapon have, used as a target for training.
source: G. B. Shustikov and V. G. Fedorov, Opredelenie modelnykh kharakteristik sportsmenov v fekhtovanii na shpagakh [Determining Model Characteristics of Épée Fencers] (2022), pp. 502 to 504
status: library

Russian sport science calls them modelnye kharakteristiki, and builds on earlier work on models of the strongest athletes and on the model-target approach. Shustikov and Fedorov cite a scheme of five model types for combat sports, which includes the model of the strongest athlete and predictive models that can even model the refereeing of bouts. For fencing they build the model of the strongest athlete from partial models: technical and tactical equipment, physical preparation, and psychophysiological and psychological readiness. Whatever the model, they stress monitoring as many measures as possible, so that the coach gets feedback and can correct training. Their worked example is the [[Épée Success Model]]. See also [[Physical Preparation]] and [[Talent Identification]].

## Épée Success Model
short: A points score that compares an épée fencer with the qualities coaches rate most important for success.
source: G. B. Shustikov and V. G. Fedorov, Opredelenie modelnykh kharakteristik sportsmenov v fekhtovanii na shpagakh [Determining Model Characteristics of Épée Fencers] (2022), pp. 504 to 506
status: library

Shustikov and Fedorov asked coaches to rank qualities for success in [[Épée]]. The order was: special physical preparation as a whole (mean rank 2.34), [[Speed]] of reaction (3.51), functional [[Endurance]] (5.12), psychological stability (6.76), wish to train (6.81), intellectual ability (8.54) and strength of the nervous system (10.30). Each quality was scored from 0 to 10 and weighted by a coefficient from 0.3 to 2. Of 60 fencers, 23.33 percent scored above 35 points, 58.33 percent between 25 and 35, and 18.33 percent below 25. The best fencers at the stage before top level averaged 37.11 points and did not differ significantly from top-level fencers (39.05); the rest averaged 28.73. See [[Model Characteristics]].

## Universal Style
short: A way of fencing in which a fencer changes freely between styles to suit the situation of the bout.
source: G. B. Shustikov and V. G. Fedorov, Pedagogicheskiy analiz individualnykh stiley vedeniya fekhtovalnogo boya rapiristov [Individual Bout Styles of Foil Fencers] (2023), pp. 474 to 476
status: library

Shustikov and Fedorov start from Turetsky's four styles: positional offensive, maneuvering offensive, maneuvering defensive and positional defensive (see [[Fencing Style]]). Among 16 top Russian [[Foil]] fencers, 56 percent fenced in the maneuvering offensive style and 44 percent in the maneuvering defensive style; none was positional defensive. Maneuvering offensive fencers rose more in the rankings, while maneuvering defensive fencers kept their results steadier. Over the study, 62.5 percent kept one style and 37.5 percent changed it with the situation. The authors call this ability a universal style: mobility, effective work at all distances, easy changes of action, flexible decisions, a drive for the initiative and fast use of the opponent's errors. They warn that it is hard to spot early. See [[Maneuvering]].

## Referee Error
short: A wrong decision by the referee, studied for its effect on the fencers and as the measure of a referee's skill.
source: G. B. Shustikov, V. G. Fedorov and E. A. Nechaeva, Povyshenie nadezhnosti sredstv vedeniya boya k oshibochnym deystviyam sudi v fekhtovanii na sablyakh [Making Bout Actions Reliable against Referee Errors in Sabre] (2016), pp. 260 to 263; G. B. Shustikov, V. G. Fedorov, A. V. Deev and I. V. Bondarev, Kompleksnaya podgotovka sudey po fekhtovaniyu na shpagakh v obrazovatelnom protsesse fizkulturno-sportivnogo vuza [Comprehensive Training of Épée Referees at a Sports University] (2020), p. 498
status: library

Shustikov and colleagues counted a [[Referee]] error when an expert panel judged it so, and when the fencer reacted typically, by arguing, taking off the mask without permission or demanding [[Video Refereeing]]. In 233 [[Sabre]] bouts, attacks made 74.8 percent of actions and scored 62.3 percent of the time under correct refereeing. With referee errors, attacks fell to 69.7 percent and scored 58.4 percent, and counter-offensive actions rose from 9.7 to 14.2 percent. The errors narrowed the fencers' range of actions. In [[Épée]], the hardest situation for referees was halting the bout in time at body contact, followed by missed hits on the floor and wrong calls at the side and rear limits of the piste.

## Referee Training
short: Teaching referees to make fewer errors per bout, the main measure of refereeing quality.
source: G. B. Shustikov, V. G. Fedorov, A. V. Deev and I. V. Bondarev, Kompleksnaya podgotovka sudey po fekhtovaniyu na shpagakh v obrazovatelnom protsesse fizkulturno-sportivnogo vuza [Comprehensive Training of Épée Referees at a Sports University] (2020), pp. 499 to 501
status: library

Shustikov and colleagues take the average number of errors per bout as the main criterion of a [[Referee]], and the referee's [[Referee's Subjective Difficulties|subjective difficulties]] as a supporting one. Watching [[Épée]] bouts, they found 0.12 errors per bout from international referees at World Championships and World Cups, 0.26 at Russian championships and cups, 0.51 at city and regional events, and 1.62 from beginners at unofficial events. In a two-year experiment with students, the group trained with a [[Multimedia Referee Training|multimedia computer complex]] cut its errors from 2.33 to 1.18 per bout, a drop of 49.37 percent. The control group went from 2.27 to 2.03, a change that was not significant.

## Referee's Subjective Difficulties
short: The fears and doubts a referee feels while refereeing, which are much stronger in beginners.
source: G. B. Shustikov, V. G. Fedorov, A. V. Deev and I. V. Bondarev, Kompleksnaya podgotovka sudey po fekhtovaniyu na shpagakh v obrazovatelnom protsesse fizkulturno-sportivnogo vuza [Comprehensive Training of Épée Referees at a Sports University] (2020), pp. 498 to 500
status: library

Shustikov and colleagues surveyed 15 top [[Épée]] fencers and 28 referees, 12 experienced and 16 beginners, and watched 126 bouts. Fear of refereeing top fencers was named by 93.75 percent of beginners and 25.00 percent of experienced referees. Fear of making a mistake was named by 81.25 and 54.55 percent, doubt about the fine points of complex situations by 81.25 and 18.18 percent, and fear of pressure from coaches by 43.75 percent of beginners and by none of the experienced referees. After [[Referee Training]], the beginners' range of difficulties fell from 100 to 60 percent, close to the level of experienced referees (50 percent). See [[Referee]].

## Multimedia Referee Training
short: Training and testing referees on a computer with rule questions, video of hard situations and virtual refereeing of real bouts.
source: G. B. Shustikov, V. G. Fedorov, O. V. Shalamova, V. M. Churin and A. V. Deev, Aktualizatsiya protsessa podgotovki sudey po fekhtovaniyu na osnove multimediynogo kompyuternogo kompleksa [Updating Fencing Referee Training with a Multimedia Computer Complex] (2025), pp. 175 to 179; G. B. Shustikov, V. G. Fedorov, A. V. Deev and I. V. Bondarev, Kompleksnaya podgotovka sudey po fekhtovaniyu na shpagakh v obrazovatelnom protsesse fizkulturno-sportivnogo vuza [Comprehensive Training of Épée Referees at a Sports University] (2020), p. 500
status: library

The complex first tests the referee with 15 questions on the rules, then shows video of situations that are hard to referee, in order of difficulty. In test mode the trainee picks one of four answers within a time limit. In virtual refereeing the video of a real bout stops after each excerpt and the trainee gives a decision. With 20 beginner referees, scores out of 5 rose in three months from 4.3 to 4.7 for basic knowledge and from 4.0 to 4.4 for applying the rules in hard situations, but only from 3.0 to 3.1 for virtual refereeing. The authors conclude that beginners are strongest in theory and still need real practice. See [[Referee Training]] and [[Video Refereeing]].

## Resistance to Interference
short: A fencer's ability to keep fencing well despite disturbances, such as referee errors, an audience or the opponent's unexpected blade movements.
source: G. B. Shustikov, V. G. Fedorov and E. A. Nechaeva, Povyshenie nadezhnosti sredstv vedeniya boya k oshibochnym deystviyam sudi v fekhtovanii na sablyakh [Making Bout Actions Reliable against Referee Errors in Sabre] (2016), pp. 261 to 263; L. G. Ryzhkova and V. V. Shamis, Pokazateli primeneniya atak i protivodeystviy im v sorevnovatelnoy deyatelnosti uchastnits Olimpiady-2020, fekhtuyushchikh na sablyakh [Use of Attacks and Counters to Them by Women Sabre Fencers at the 2020 Olympics] (2023), p. 39
status: library

Russian literature calls it pomekhoustoychivost. To build it against [[Referee Error]], Shustikov and colleagues used three parts with sabre fencers: self-regulation methods, repertoire lessons and lessons given in front of an audience, and training situations that model wrong refereeing. The lessons favoured actions that leave the referee little doubt: blade actions in the attack, counter-attacks with [[Opposition]], and avoiding exchanges with hits on both sides. After the experiment, measured over 62 bouts with refereeing errors, all the indicators improved and every difference was significant. Ryzhkova and Shamis ask for the same quality against early or oncoming blade movements by the opponent. See [[Psychological Preparation]].
