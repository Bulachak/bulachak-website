## Classification of Fencing Actions
short: How coaching literature sorts what fencers do in a bout: preparatory actions, and the actions that try to score a touch or stop one.
source: V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), pp. 10 to 14; D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), chapter Technique of Fencing, figures 1 and 2; Z. Czajkowski, Tactics in Fencing: Preparatory Actions (2009), p. 375; M. A. Rydnik, Sostav sredstv vedeniya poedinkov fekhtovalshchikami-rapiristami [Means of Conducting Bouts in Foil and Motor Types] (2010), p. 95
status: library

Arkadyev splits all combat actions into main actions and [[Preparatory Action|preparatory actions]]. Main actions are offensive, defensive or mixed. Tyshler's textbook lists, next to the preparatory actions, the [[Attack]], the [[Parry]], the [[Riposte]], the counter-parry and the [[Counter-Riposte]], and a group of counter-offensive actions: the [[Counter-Attack]], the [[Remise]] and the hit from a straight arm (point in line). Czajkowski makes the same first split: actual actions, meant to score or to stop a hit, and preparatory actions, which only make them easier. Main actions are also sorted by intention: see [[Second Intention]].

## Preparatory Action
short: An action that does not try to score or stop a touch, but prepares the action that will.
source: D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), chapter Tactics of Fencing and Terminology, pp. 64 to 73; V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), pp. 14 to 20; Z. Czajkowski, Tactics in Fencing: Preparatory Actions (2009), pp. 375 to 377; M. A. Rydnik, Sredstva vedeniya poedinkov kak osnova operezhayushchego modelirovaniya [Means of Conducting Bouts as the Basis of Advance Modelling] (2009), p. 83
status: library

Tyshler names four tasks: [[Reconnaissance]] of the opponent, concealment of one's own intentions (see [[Tactical Deception]]), [[Invitation|invitations]] that prompt a chosen action, and hindrances that make it harder for the opponent to react. They are carried out through play with the weapon, [[Maneuvering]], imitated attacks or parries, false attacks or parries with a real threat (see [[False Attack]]), and actions that cannot score. Czajkowski gives examples of hindering: holding the point in line against an opponent who likes compound attacks, or keeping the blade away from one who likes to take it. He also notes that some great fencers use few scoring actions but prepare them with great variety. Rydnik's notation of more than 650 foil bouts from 2007 to 2009 found more preparatory actions than in 2004 and 2005, with bouts moving toward positional play.

## Reconnaissance
short: Preparatory moves made to learn how the opponent reacts, what they intend and what state they are in.
avoid: scouting; scout
source: V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), pp. 14 to 15; Z. Czajkowski, Tactics in Fencing: Preparatory Actions (2009), p. 375; D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), Terminology, pp. 64 to 73
status: library

Soviet coaching literature calls it razvedka. The fencer tests the opponent's defence with the start of an attack, or with a whole attack that goes less far than a real one, and tests the reaction to feints and beats by making them on their own. Typical questions are when the opponent attacks, which actions they like, and whether, taken by surprise, they parry or counter-attack. Czajkowski adds that a general picture also comes from watching the opponent warm up, take a lesson and fence others (see [[Video Analysis]]). The opponent's answer is [[Tactical Deception]]: giving nothing away, or showing a false reaction on purpose.

> Why did you keep making half attacks in the first minute?
> To see what she does with them. Now I know which parry to deceive.

## Tactical Deception
short: Giving the opponent a false picture of your intentions, state or abilities, so that they make the mistake you have prepared for.
source: V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), pp. 16 and 38 to 40; Z. Czajkowski, Tactics in Fencing: Preparatory Actions (2009), p. 375; D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), chapter Tactics of Fencing and Terminology, pp. 64 to 73
status: library

Its passive part is concealment, which Soviet textbooks call maskirovka: hiding one's intentions and state. Czajkowski describes concealment as not betraying an intention by needless gestures, and misleading as the active form: showing one line of action, then using a different one. Arkadyev stresses that timing matters more than how convincing the movement looks. A false move made when the opponent does not expect it works even if poorly made, and the most truthful one fails if it is expected. He separates direct deception, which draws the action the situation seems to offer, from indirect deception, which lets the opponent spot the trap so that they answer it in a way that has also been prepared for. See also [[Invitation]] and [[Second Intention]].

## Invitation
short: A position or movement that prompts the opponent to make a particular action, which you are ready to meet.
source: D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), Terminology, pp. 64 to 73; V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), pp. 16 to 17; Z. Czajkowski, Tactics in Fencing: Preparatory Actions (2009), p. 376
status: library

Soviet coaching literature calls it vyzov. Opening a target, or holding the blade in a chosen engagement, can draw an [[Attack]] or a [[Counter-Attack]] that the fencer parries and answers with a [[Riposte]]. Arkadyev and Czajkowski treat it as the main tool for directing the opponent's game: the fencer who draws an action knows what is coming and can start the answer earlier. It also works on mood: against a strong defender, acting timid can draw a rash attack that is easy to parry. The answer to an invitation is to see through it, go along with the first movement, then avoid the prepared reply.

> He keeps leaving his arm open.
> Then ask yourself why. If it is an invitation, let him think you took it.

## Maneuvering
short: Moving forward and back on the piste to gain ground, find your own distance or upset the opponent's. In parafencing the chairs are fixed, so this footwork is not available.
source: D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), Terminology, pp. 64 to 73; V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), pp. 17 to 20; Z. Czajkowski, Tactics in Fencing: Preparatory Actions (2009), p. 376; Wheelchair Fencing (undated overview in the library, file 554620003)
status: library

Tyshler defines maneuvering as deep movement across the field of play. Arkadyev and Czajkowski use it to push the opponent toward the end of the piste, to find the distance from which one's own attack works, and to hide the start of an attack inside steady play with the feet and the blade. Each fencer fights for "their own" distance, which depends on the length and speed of their attacks and the strength of their parries. The wheelchair fencing overview in the library notes that the chairs are fixed to the floor, so the fencers cannot move back and forth. In [[Parafencing]] the [[Fencing Distance]] is set before the bout and the chairs stay on the [[Frame]]. See also [[Trunk Lean]].

> Can I still fight for my own distance in the chair?
> Not with your feet. The distance is set before the bout and stays fixed.

## Phrase
short: One continuous exchange in a bout, from the first action to the last. It can be a single action or a long series of actions and answers.
source: D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), chapter Tactics of Fencing and Terminology, pp. 64 to 73
status: library

Soviet textbooks call it skhvatka: one episode of the [[Bout]], made of actions that follow without a break. A phrase may be one act (one action and the opponent's response) or two, three or many acts. Tyshler notes that tactical uncertainty grows as a phrase develops from a single action into a long exchange. The textbook also asks that phrases ending in a [[Touch]] and phrases ending without one be recorded separately in analysis. For how the referee reads the last phrase at foil and sabre, see [[Priority]].

## Simple Attack
short: An attack made in one tempo, with one movement of the weapon: direct, by disengage, or by cut-over.
source: D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), Terminology, pp. 64 to 73; G. B. Shustikov and V. G. Fedorov, Sistematizatsiya bazovykh terminologicheskikh ponyatiy v sovremennom fekhtovanii [Systematising Basic Terms in Modern Fencing] (2014), p. 202; V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), pp. 21 to 22; Z. Czajkowski, Tactics in Fencing: Preparatory Actions (2009), p. 373
status: library

A simple [[Attack]] goes straight to the target, or by a disengage (going round the opponent's blade, guard or arm into another sector), or by a cut-over (passing over the opponent's point). Tyshler applies the same one-tempo rule to the simple riposte, counter-attack and remise. Arkadyev argues that the simple attack shows tactical skill most fully, because it succeeds only when its moment is exactly right (see [[Choice of Time]]). A fencer who lands simple attacks also makes the opponent stop counter-attacking, which opens the way for a [[Compound Attack]]. Czajkowski counts the first intention simple attack among the most frequent successful actions of top fencers, though it is difficult to carry out.

## Compound Attack
short: An attack made in two or more tempi: with feints, with an action on the blade, or with both.
source: D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), Terminology, pp. 64 to 73; G. B. Shustikov and V. G. Fedorov, Sistematizatsiya bazovykh terminologicheskikh ponyatiy v sovremennom fekhtovanii [Systematising Basic Terms in Modern Fencing] (2014), p. 202; V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), pp. 21 to 22 and 27 to 29
status: library

Soviet textbooks call these complex attacks. There are three kinds: the attack with [[Feint|feints]], the attack with an [[Action on the Blade|action on the blade]], and the combined attack, which joins an action on the blade to feints before the hit. Arkadyev says the preliminary movements make the approach safe and open a target. He advises alternating: the parry and riposte is the best answer to a simple attack, and the [[Counter-Attack]] is strong against a compound one, so simple attacks should meet an opponent who is trying to counter-attack and compound attacks one who is trying to parry. See also [[Simple Attack]].

> My feint attack worked twice, then she stop-hit it.
> She was waiting for the feint. Show her a simple attack next.

## Feint
short: A threatening movement of the weapon that makes the opponent react with a parry.
source: D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), Terminology, pp. 64 to 73; V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), pp. 27 to 28
status: library

Arkadyev gives the feint two jobs: to get through the approach safely, by keeping the opponent busy defending, and to place the blades so that the final hit can pass. A second or third feint delays the opponent's final parry further. A feint the opponent expects works against the attacker, because it invites a [[Counter-Attack]]. Tyshler's textbook names the one-two (a feint by disengage, then a hit by disengage into another sector, to deceive a direct parry) and the doublé (feint and hit into the same sector, to deceive a circular parry). Arkadyev also describes deception by a pause, which some call syncopation: breaking the usual rhythm of the attack with a pause and a fast finish. Feints belong to the [[Compound Attack]].

## Action on the Blade
short: Any action of your blade on the opponent's blade: beats, takings, pressure, or holding the engagement.
source: V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), pp. 28 to 29; D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), Terminology, pp. 64 to 73; G. B. Shustikov and V. G. Fedorov, Sistematizatsiya bazovykh terminologicheskikh ponyatiy v sovremennom fekhtovanii [Systematising Basic Terms in Modern Fencing] (2014), p. 203
status: library

Inside an attack, Arkadyev gives it two tasks: to approach with the least risk of a [[Counter-Attack]], and to place the blades well for the hit. On its own, an action on the blade can serve [[Reconnaissance]] or an [[Invitation]]. An unexpected [[Beat]] or [[Prise de Fer|taking]] protects against a straight counter-attack, but an opponent who expects it can deceive it by disengage, so the direction of the blade should be hidden as well as the moment. When the direction itself is unexpected, the surprise moves from the start of the attack to the blade. For what happens when an attacker misses the blade, see [[Priority]].

## Beat
short: A sharp blow of your blade on the opponent's blade, to shake it or knock it off the target.
source: D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), Terminology, pp. 64 to 73; G. B. Shustikov and V. G. Fedorov, Sistematizatsiya bazovykh terminologicheskikh ponyatiy v sovremennom fekhtovanii [Systematising Basic Terms in Modern Fencing] (2014), p. 203; V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), p. 37; Z. Czajkowski, Tactics in Fencing: Preparatory Actions (2009), p. 376
status: library

Soviet textbooks call it batman. Tyshler sorts beats by path: direct (sideways), semicircular (up or down) and circular (back to the starting line). He also sorts them by manner: the percussive beat, which meets the blade, and the passing beat, which slides along it and then goes round the point. At [[Sabre]] a beat can be made with the edge or the back of the blade. Arkadyev and Czajkowski both note that very strong beats irritate some opponents and spoil their concentration, which makes the beat a [[Preparatory Action]] as well. Where the beat lands matters at foil and sabre: see [[Priority]].

## Prise de Fer
short: Taking the opponent's blade with force while in contact with it, to control it before the hit. It includes the bind and the envelopment.
source: D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), Terminology, pp. 64 to 73; G. B. Shustikov and V. G. Fedorov, Sistematizatsiya bazovykh terminologicheskikh ponyatiy v sovremennom fekhtovanii [Systematising Basic Terms in Modern Fencing] (2014), p. 203; V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), pp. 28 to 29
status: library

Soviet textbooks call it zakhvat: a forceful action on the opponent's blade while the blades are engaged. Like the [[Beat]], it can be direct, semicircular or circular. The envelopment, zavyazyvanie, is a kind of taking that carries the opponent's blade through different planes. Arkadyev advises hiding the direction of the taking, because an opponent who expects it can deceive it by disengage. A lighter relative is pressure, which keeps control of the opponent's blade while waiting for their reaction. See also [[Opposition]] and [[Action on the Blade]].

## Attack on Preparation
short: An attack made on an opponent who is moving forward to prepare their own attack.
source: D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), chapter Technique of Fencing and Terminology, pp. 64 to 73; G. B. Shustikov and V. G. Fedorov, Sistematizatsiya bazovykh terminologicheskikh ponyatiy v sovremennom fekhtovanii [Systematising Basic Terms in Modern Fencing] (2014), p. 203; V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), pp. 22 to 23
status: library

Tyshler's textbook sorts the [[Attack]] by the moment it starts: the basic attack that opens the exchange, the attack on preparation, the return attack (on an opponent retreating after a failed attack of their own), and the [[Renewed Attack]]. Arkadyev explains why the attack on preparation works: an opponent coming forward is busy with their own plan and is not expecting to be attacked. In the textbook's criteria of preparedness, attacks on preparation are the most informative kind of attack at [[Foil]] and are also valued at [[Épée]] and [[Sabre]]. The action that forestalls it is [[Counter-Time]].

## Renewed Attack
short: A new attack on an opponent who defended, by parry, retreat or evasion, without trying to riposte.
source: D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), Terminology, pp. 64 to 73; G. B. Shustikov and V. G. Fedorov, Sistematizatsiya bazovykh terminologicheskikh ponyatiy v sovremennom fekhtovanii [Systematising Basic Terms in Modern Fencing] (2014), p. 203; V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), pp. 25 to 26
status: library

Soviet textbooks call it povtornaya ataka. Taken on its own it is an ordinary [[Attack]]; it is named by what came just before. Arkadyev says the first attack, without meaning to, prepares the surprise of the second, and that renewed attacks are usually improvised. A planned form follows a [[False Attack]] as part of [[Second Intention]], and its surprise comes from the opponent's attention being fixed on the first attack. Arkadyev counts the doubled hit, made closer and against an opponent who has parried, as a kind of renewed attack (compare [[Remise]]).

## False Attack
short: A movement that looks like an attack but is meant only to draw a reaction that you then exploit.
source: V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), pp. 25 to 26; D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), chapter Technique of Fencing and Terminology, pp. 64 to 73; Z. Czajkowski, Tactics in Fencing: Preparatory Actions (2009), p. 375
status: library

Tyshler's textbook defines false attacks as shorter than needed to reach the target, and false parries as deeper than needed. Arkadyev lists their uses: pushing the opponent back, [[Reconnaissance]], play with distance, and the opening of a [[Second Intention]] action. A false attack is also a fairly safe way to come closer, because it can turn into a real attack if the opponent counter-attacks. Czajkowski notes that one false attack can serve several aims at once: testing the reaction, revealing the opponent's intention, misleading them and gaining distance. The textbook's criteria rate highly, at foil, the false attack finished with a counter-parry and [[Counter-Riposte]].

## Second Intention
short: A planned action whose first, false threat is meant to draw a particular answer, so that the touch is scored on the next action.
source: V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), pp. 13 to 14 and 26 to 27; D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), Terminology, pp. 64 to 73; Z. Czajkowski, Tactics in Fencing: Preparatory Actions (2009), pp. 375 to 376
status: library

Arkadyev sorts main actions by intention. First intention actions are true attempts to hit, and they are so common that fencers rarely name them. In second intention, the first threat only draws a set reaction, such as a parry and riposte or a [[Counter-Attack]], and the touch comes in the next action: a riposte on the counter-attack, a [[Counter-Riposte]] or a [[Renewed Attack]]. A third group aims at any of several reactions, with the fencer ready for each. The [[False Attack]] that is meant to draw a counter-attack must be made truthfully, not by surprise, so that the counter-attack really comes. Czajkowski stresses that second intention counter-time and parry counter-riposte are real scoring actions, not preparatory ones.

> I attacked short on purpose and she still did not counter.
> Then your false attack looked false. She has to believe it.

## Counter-Time
short: An action against the opponent's counter-attack or attack on preparation: you draw it out or expect it, and hit first.
source: D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), Terminology, pp. 64 to 73; V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), pp. 12 and 27; Z. Czajkowski, Tactics in Fencing: Preparatory Actions (2009), p. 376
status: library

Soviet textbooks call it kontrtemp, and the 1997 textbook defines it as an offensive action that forestalls the opponent's [[Attack on Preparation]]. Arkadyev describes the counter-time attack: an attack in one tempo, started from long distance so that the opponent thinks they can counter-attack the approach, which still reaches them as they come forward to do so. He uses it to approach safely and to remove counter-attacks from the opponent's game. In his chain of actions and answers, a counter-time attack is one answer to the [[Counter-Attack]], feints inside the drawn action answer second intention, and counter-time and the [[Remise]] answer those feints in turn. Czajkowski counts second intention counter-time among the real scoring actions (see [[Second Intention]]).

## Remise
short: A new hit made straight after meeting the opponent's parry, to arrive before their riposte.
source: D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), chapter Technique of Fencing and Terminology, pp. 64 to 73; V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), pp. 12 to 13 and 25; M. A. Rydnik, Sostav sredstv vedeniya poedinkov fekhtovalshchikami-rapiristami [Means of Conducting Bouts in Foil and Motor Types] (2010), p. 95
status: library

Tyshler's textbook and Rydnik group the remise with the [[Counter-Attack]] as counter-offensive actions, and the textbook lists it among the answers to a [[Riposte]]. It is a simple, one-tempo action, made with or without premeditation. At foil and sabre a remise never has priority of its own. Arkadyev says that in all counter-actions, remises included, the fencer must win a fencing tempo (see [[Fencing Time]] and [[Priority]]), and that a successful remise needs very fine reading of the moment. In the textbook's criteria for [[Épée]], the use of remises is a sign of good preparation.

## Counter-Riposte
short: The hit made by the attacker after parrying the opponent's riposte.
source: D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), chapter Technique of Fencing and Terminology, pp. 64 to 73; V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), pp. 12 and 26 to 27
status: library

Tyshler's textbook pairs it with the counter-parry (kontrzashchita), the parry of the opponent's [[Riposte]]. The counter-riposte follows directly after that parry. Arkadyev describes a [[Second Intention]] attack built for it: a [[False Attack]] that ends with a convincing hitting movement, a parry of the riposte, then the counter-riposte. He calls it riskier than drawing a counter-attack, because a false attack made too deep lets the riposte land. In the textbook's criteria, counter-parries and counter-ripostes rate highly at foil, at sabre, and at épée in close fighting.

## Stop Hit
short: A counter-attack made into the opponent's movement before or during their attack, meant to arrive a fencing time ahead.
source: V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), pp. 24 to 25; D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), chapter Technique of Fencing and Terminology, pp. 64 to 73
status: library

Arkadyev sorts the [[Counter-Attack]] by its moment. The stop counter-attack, or stop hit, is made on the forward movement of an opponent about to attack; it is so close to an attack that tenths of a second decide which one it is. The counter-attack in tempo is made on an attack already under way, on its approach or on one of its preliminary movements. The meeting attack does not try to gain a tempo: at [[Épée]] it may aim for a [[Double Touch]], and at all weapons it can be made with [[Opposition]] or with an evasion of the body. Arkadyev advises hiding the intention to counter-attack, and watching for false attacks that invite it (see [[Second Intention]]). The rule book uses the name stop hit for any counter-attack into an attack and judges it by [[Fencing Time]].

## Opposition
short: Using force on the opponent's weapon to close one sector of your target while you make your own hit.
source: D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), chapter Technique of Fencing and Terminology, pp. 64 to 73; V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), p. 25
status: library

Tyshler's textbook defines opposition this way. It lists it as one of four ways an offensive action gets its result, with gaining a tempo, simultaneity and evasion. Arkadyev places the counter-attack with opposition between defence and attack, because it does both at once. The textbook's criteria name counter-attacks with opposition and ripostes with opposition at [[Foil]] and [[Épée]]. The rule book describes the stop hit with opposition under [[Counter-Attack]]. See also [[Prise de Fer]].

## General Parry
short: A parry that sweeps across every path the attacking blade could take. It starts before the target of the attack is known.
source: V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), pp. 29 to 32; D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), chapter Technique of Fencing and Terminology, pp. 64 to 73
status: library

Soviet coaching literature calls it obobshchayushchaya zashchita, the generalising parry. Arkadyev contrasts it with the specific parry, which acts on a blade already seen. The general parry starts early, but its wide movement can meet the blade with the wrong part of one's own. Arkadyev also lists the interception (a parry that acts on the opponent's blade like a beat), the false parry, the second intention parry, and the complex general parry of two joined movements, which he trusts only against fencers who love feints. Tyshler's criteria rate circular parries highly at foil and at épée, where the textbook calls them general. Arkadyev insists that a [[Parry]] be followed by a [[Riposte]]: parrying a real attempt without answering wastes the best moment of the bout.

> I parried it clean and then froze.
> That parry was your best chance in the bout. Riposte every time.

## Choice of Time
short: Finding or creating the moment to start an action, so that it catches the opponent unready. Czajkowski calls it the sense of timing, or sense of surprise.
source: D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), chapter Tactics of Fencing and chapter Components of Preparedness; V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), pp. 12 to 13, 15 to 16 and 40 to 41; Z. Czajkowski, Tactics in Fencing: Preparatory Actions (2009), pp. 371 to 373
status: library

Tyshler's textbook lists the choice and preparation of the moment for an attack, or for an answer to one, as a traditional tactical category. Experts rate it, with the sense of distance and bout experience, among the most informative signs of tactical preparedness. Arkadyev puts the main tactical skill in acting by surprise. Good moments come when the opponent turns attention to preparing their own attack, rests for an instant, or is lulled by an even rhythm that is then broken by a half tempo. Surprise can be full, when the action was not in the opponent's forecast at all, or partial, when the action was expected but its content was not. He puts one fencing tempo, the time of one simple action, at about a quarter of a second (see [[Fencing Time]]). See also [[Attack on Preparation]].

## Premeditated Action
short: An action the fencer planned before the phrase, then waited for or created the moment to use. Its opposite is the improvised action.
source: D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), chapter Technique of Fencing and Terminology, pp. 64 to 73; V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), pp. 10 to 11; Z. Czajkowski, Tactics in Fencing: Preparatory Actions (2009), pp. 373 to 374
status: library

Tyshler's textbook names three degrees of readiness: premeditated, improvised (made on an unexpected cue), and premeditated then improvised (a planned start with an improvised finish). Basic and renewed attacks are mostly premeditated. Ripostes, counter-attacks, remises and attacks on preparation are used both ways, and parries must be ready for both. Arkadyev adds forced, instinctive and reflex actions, and warns that reflexes formed by drilling one pattern too much can trap a fencer in a losing response. Czajkowski links the balance to temperament: a fast, lively fencer can build on improvisation, while a calmer one relies on observation, premeditated actions and [[Second Intention]]. See also [[Tactical Thinking]].

## Sense of Combat
short: A fencer's specialised ability to act correctly in sudden situations. It is built from the sense of the blade, the sense of distance and the sense of time.
source: D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), chapter Tactics of Fencing and Terminology, pp. 64 to 73; L. G. Ryzhkova, Spetsializirovannye takticheskie umeniya kak fundament dlya formirovaniya chuvstva boya u fekhtovalshchikov na rapirakh [Specialised Tactical Skills as the Basis of the Sense of Combat in Foil Fencers] (2016), pp. 176 to 180; V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), pp. 34 to 35; Z. Czajkowski, Characteristics of Contemporary Fencing (2011), p. 56
status: library

Soviet coaching literature calls it chuvstvo boya. Tyshler's textbook builds it from three senses: the sense of the weapon (quick and accurate touch through the blade, which Czajkowski calls sentiment de fer), the sense of distance, and the sense of time, meaning the judgement of very short intervals. Arkadyev describes a fencer with a sense of combat as able to improvise in a flash, acting before the moment is fully understood. Ryzhkova set out, with foil coaches, the order in which the skills under it are built. First come conditioned actions on a simple reaction (attack, parry, riposte, then counter-attack and remise). Then come actions with choice, then switching from one action to another, and last the anticipation of distance, of the moment and of changes in the opponent's blade and movement. The first two rest on the [[Simple Reaction]] and the [[Choice Reaction]]. See also [[Tactical Thinking]], [[Anticipation Reaction]] and [[Choice of Time]].

> How do I train that? It sounds like talent.
> It is built in steps: set responses first, then choice, then switching.

## Tactical Thinking
short: Thinking that happens inside the action of the bout: reading the opponent, planning, setting traps and seeing through theirs, all under time pressure.
source: V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), pp. 33 to 41; D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), chapter Tactics of Fencing and chapter Psychogram of Fencing; Z. Czajkowski, Tactics in Fencing: Preparatory Actions (2009), p. 373
status: library

Arkadyev lists its objects: observing the opponent, making a plan for the [[Bout]], recognising the opponent's reactions and intentions, setting specific tasks, misleading the opponent (see [[Tactical Deception]]) and exposing their deceptions. It runs on two tracks at once, attack and defence. He says that being late in understanding the bout costs more than slow movements, and Czajkowski makes the same point. Memory of the bout lets a fencer anticipate the next move, for example by counting how often an action has already been shown. A plan sets one's strengths against the opponent's weaknesses, but should be changed during the bout when it proves wrong. See also [[Tactical Preparation]].

## Fencing Style
short: The typical way a fencer builds bouts, shaped by temperament, training and favourite actions.
source: D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), chapter Tactics of Fencing and Terminology, pp. 64 to 73; V. A. Arkadyev, Taktika v fekhtovanii [Tactics in Fencing] (1969), pp. 47 to 49; Z. Czajkowski, Tactics in Fencing: Preparatory Actions (2009), pp. 373 to 374; L. G. Ryzhkova, Takticheskaya informatsiya v trenirovke fekhtovalshchika [Tactical Information in Fencer Training] (2009), pp. 68 to 69
status: library

Tyshler's textbook describes bouts in three pairs: offensive or defensive, maneuvering or positional, and fast (a fight for the initiative with little preparation) or waiting (long preparation). Ryzhkova calls these bout models and links them to what psychology calls style. Arkadyev sorts fencers, schematically, into active types, who drive the bout and create the opponent's errors, and passive types, who give away the initiative. He also names the tactical dominant: a steady preference for one action or a small group of them. Czajkowski warns against copying champions blindly and asks coaches to help each fencer find a style that suits their temperament, build and personality. See also [[Maneuvering]] and [[Premeditated Action]].

## Tactical Adequacy
short: Whether the action a fencer chose fitted the situation and the opponent's intention, judged action by action in bout analysis.
source: L. G. Ryzhkova, Adekvatnost takticheskikh namereniy i primeneniya deystviy v sorevnovatelnykh poedinkakh [Adequacy of Tactical Intentions and Actions in Bouts of Top Fencers] (2014), pp. 146 to 150; D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), chapter Technique of Fencing
status: library

Tyshler's textbook names three measures of the actions in bouts: volume, effectiveness and tactical adequacy. Ryzhkova applied them to video of the top eight foil fencers at the 2013 European Championships. An action counted as adequate when it fitted the situation and the rules: it hit an opponent who was late with their answer, or the [[Referee]] gave it [[Priority]] or a lead of one fencing tempo. The winner's inadequate actions cost a touch far less often than those of the fencers placed sixth to eighth, which Ryzhkova reads as greater psychological stability. See [[Video Analysis]].

> We won the bout, so why go through it again?
> Because some touches came from the wrong choice. The video shows which ones.
