## Individual Lesson
short: One coach works with one fencer, playing the opponent, to build actions and the choices behind them.
source: Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing], textbook (1997), chapter "Organisation and Methods of Training"; Green, The Classical Foil Fencing Lesson (2016), pp. 50 to 53; Soter and Schliep, USFCA Epee Moniteur Workshop syllabus, Center for Blade Arts (2025)
status: library

Tyshler's textbook names the individual lesson as one of the main forms of fencing training, and for qualified fencers it often carries most of the technical and tactical work. The coach plays partner or opponent and can create sudden situations that a training partner cannot. Green's model lesson has four parts: an introduction, a warm-up, a main body (review, demonstration, explanation, progressive drills and defence against the action) and a conclusion with a summary and the subject of the next lesson. The classical masters he reviewed gave lessons of 15 to 45 minutes, and the USFCA syllabus separates technical, tactical and mixed lessons. See also [[Cue]], [[Error Correction]] and [[Combat Lesson]].

> Can we work on my parry today?
> Yes. Warm-up first, then the parry, then I will attack you and you defend.

## Group Lesson
short: A coach trains several fencers at once on one theme, mostly through exercises in pairs.
source: Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing], textbook (1997), chapter "Forms of Organising Sessions"; Green, The Classical Foil Fencing Lesson (2016), pp. 60 to 61; Soter and Schliep, USFCA Epee Moniteur Workshop syllabus (2025)
status: library

In Tyshler's textbook the group lesson is the main form of training in the early years, and exercises with a partner are its core when one coach has more than five or six fencers. Green's outline for a group lesson runs: introduction, warm-up, review of the last lesson, main body, one extension of the technique (defence against it, tactical use, a variant or speed work), bouting if time allows, and a conclusion. In the master-pupil variant, fencers are paired and one plays the coach for the other; partners of similar level swap roles after a set number of repetitions. See [[Paired Exercise]] and [[Individual Lesson]].

## Combat Lesson
short: An individual lesson fenced like a bout: the fencer probes, chooses and attacks, and the coach punishes mistakes.
source: Green, The Classical Foil Fencing Lesson (2016), pp. 58 to 59
status: library

Green builds this lesson from two lessons Luigi Barbasetti used to prepare pupils for the assault. Both start at the normal distance for a bout, and the fencer uses [[False Attack|false attacks]], [[Feint|feints]] or attempts on the blade as [[Reconnaissance]] to read the coach before choosing the moment for a real [[Attack]]. The coach may attack when the fencer loses focus, and may [[Counter-Attack]] an error in preparation. At first any well-formed action at the right time and distance should succeed; the coach raises speed and difficulty as the fencer improves. Green runs it as a [[Silent Lesson]], with cues given by blade and movement only.

## Silent Lesson
short: An individual lesson in which the coach gives every cue without words, through blade and movement.
source: Green, The Classical Foil Fencing Lesson (2016), pp. 57 to 58
status: library

Green traces the silent lesson to Felix Grave (1934) and to a master who still taught it at a United States coaches conference in 2005. With no spoken commands, the fencer must solve each problem alone. The coach can train quick adaptation: after several clean repetitions of an attack, the coach adds a [[Parry]] and [[Riposte]], and the fencer must spot the change and answer it, for example with a feint that deceives the parry. It also trains taking the initiative, because the coach stops leading and waits for the fencer to start. See also [[Cue]].

## Cue
short: The signal a coach gives in a lesson, by blade, movement, word or sign, to call for the fencer's action.
source: Green, The Classical Foil Fencing Lesson (2016), pp. 49 to 50 and 57; Soter and Schliep, USFCA Epee Moniteur Workshop syllabus (2025)
status: library

Green finds cueing in classical texts from Rondelle (1894) on, because complex actions cannot be drilled unless the coach makes the opening moves. He gives three guidelines: larger cues for newer fencers and smaller, faster ones for experienced fencers; cues that look like a real opponent's action; and cues that cannot be misread. He notes that spoken commands are limited, since a real bout has no such signals and they leave the fencer no room for judgement. The USFCA syllabus lists distance, visual cues and verbal or sign instructions among the core skills of a coach in the [[Individual Lesson]].

> Why did you stop calling out the parries?
> Nobody calls them in a bout. Watch my blade instead.

## Error Correction
short: How a coach fixes faults in a lesson: let the fencer try again first, then correct one thing at a time.
source: Green, The Classical Foil Fencing Lesson (2016), pp. 50 and 59; Soter and Schliep, USFCA Epee Moniteur Workshop syllabus (2025)
status: library

Green reports that some classical masters corrected every error, while Rondelle, Cass and Terrone warned that constant correction of small faults holds a fencer back. His approach: allow several repetitions so the fencer can correct themselves; if that fails, give one correction at a time, look for the root cause, and keep to faults in the action being taught. In the [[Combat Lesson]], a wrong action is corrected by frustrating it with a riposte or counter-attack. The USFCA syllabus asks coaches in training to show error correction throughout every assessed [[Individual Lesson]].

## Verbal Instruction Method
short: In the individual lesson the coach tells the fencer what the coach will do, and the fencer chooses the answer and the moment.
source: Ryzhkova, The coach's verbal instruction method in the individual lesson, Uchenye zapiski universiteta imeni P.F. Lesgafta (2019), no. 4 (170), pp. 277 to 281; Tyshler and Ryzhkova, Fekhtovanie. Tekhniko-takticheskaya i funktsionalnaya trenirovka [Fencing: Technical-Tactical and Functional Training] (2010), pp. 22 to 23
status: library

Ryzhkova's method (verbalnaya ustanovka trenera, the coach's verbal instruction) recreates competition situations in the [[Individual Lesson]]. The coach announces a tactical intention, for example a [[Counter-Attack]] to the sword arm or no blade action at all, and the fencer chooses which attack to use, picks the moment and distance, and finishes with a [[Touch]]. In her one-season study of 32 [[Épée]] fencers aged 13 and 14, the group trained this way completed the tasks faster and earned higher expert ratings than a control group. Tyshler and Ryzhkova also describe the reverse version: the fencer states the planned action, and the coach recreates the situation for it.

> I will counter-attack to your arm. Choose your attack.
> Then I will beat in fourth and hit the arm.

## Paired Exercise
short: Two fencers drill agreed actions together, each helping the other; the main tool of the group lesson in the early years.
source: Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing], textbook (1997), chapters "Content of Exercises with a Partner" and "Specific Training Methods"; Ryzhkova and Kravtsov, Distributing types of specialised activity by psychological strain, Teoriya i praktika fizicheskoy kultury (2021), no. 12, pp. 25 to 26; Movshovich, Trenirovka yunykh fekhtovalschikov [Training Young Fencers] (2013); Green, The Classical Foil Fencing Lesson (2016), p. 54
status: library

Tyshler's textbook makes the paired exercise the main form of work in early groups; it gives way to the [[Individual Lesson]] as fencers improve but stays in use for technique. Ryzhkova and Kravtsov describe three levels: exercises with pre-arranged actions, exercises with choice and switching, and free exercises with no agreed ending (the Russian term is kontry). Green adds the action-and-counter format, in which partners swap roles so both learn the action and learn to recognise it. Movshovich found that by about age 13, paired exercises, individual lessons and bouting together fill about 70 percent of a session. See also [[Group Lesson]] and [[Psychological Strain]].

## Solo Exercise
short: Work a fencer does alone: movement drills, blade control, target practice or shadow bouts, without a live opponent.
source: Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing], textbook (1997), chapter "Specific Training Methods"; Ryzhkova and Kravtsov, Distributing types of specialised activity by psychological strain (2021), p. 26; Movshovich, Trenirovka yunykh fekhtovalschikov [Training Young Fencers] (2013); Green, The Classical Foil Fencing Lesson (2016), p. 62
status: library

Tyshler's textbook calls this training without an opponent, used to learn basic positions and weapon movements; work at targets and training devices is training with a conditional opponent, which sharpens judgement of distance and accuracy. Ryzhkova and Kravtsov rate solo movement drills at 1 on their ten-point scale of [[Psychological Strain]], and target work or shadow bouts at 2. Movshovich found that by about age 16, solo and apparatus work serve mainly as a warm-up. Green cites Rondelle, who had fencers practise against a wall pad before each lesson to improve accuracy. Compare [[Paired Exercise]]; for solo sessions done at home from a coach's plan, see [[Self-Directed Training]].

## Training Bout
short: A bout fenced in training: free, under a coach's task, with agreed actions, or for a recorded score.
source: Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing], textbook (1997), chapters "Forms of Organising Sessions" and "Situations and Conditions of Training Bouts"; Ryzhkova and Kravtsov (2021), p. 26; Ryzhkova, Petrenko, Pakhomova and Nikolaeva, Comparing psychological strain of bouts in training and in competition, Teoriya i praktika fizicheskoy kultury (2023), no. 5, pp. 31 to 32; Nikiforova, Markin and Udovichenko, Preparing qualified fencers for the main competitions of the season, Uchenye zapiski universiteta imeni P.F. Lesgafta (2026), no. 2 (252), p. 141
status: library

Tyshler's textbook describes bouting practice (boevaya praktika) as a session of two to four hours filled with series of bouts, with or without counting the result; it builds fitness and tests the fencer's set of actions. Before a [[Bout]] the coach can set a task drawn from real competition conditions, such as a critical score or a time limit. Nikiforova and colleagues move from bouts with pre-arranged actions to bouts with a free ending, and report that bouts for a result fill 30 to 40 percent of training time before the first competitions of the season. Ryzhkova and Kravtsov rate a bout with a coach's task at 7 and a scored training bout at 8 on their scale of [[Psychological Strain]], and Ryzhkova and colleagues found that scored training bouts help fencers adapt to the stress of competition.

> Do we count this one?
> Yes, it goes in the record. Fence it like a competition bout.

## Technical Preparation
short: Training that builds a fencer's set of actions and refines the movements that carry them out.
source: Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing], textbook (1997), chapter "Technical Preparation"; Gamaliy and Shevchuk, Technical-tactical preparation of fencers at different stages of long-term development (year not stated, written before the 2016 Games), pp. 147 to 150
status: library

Tyshler's textbook defines technical preparation as mastering a set of fencing actions and refining how they are performed; it also lays the ground for tactical and psychological work. Technical exercises belong in every session and lead at the start of each season. Alongside technique the fencer builds the specialised feel for weapon, distance and time (see [[Sense of Combat]]). Gamaliy and Shevchuk used 3D motion capture to compare top [[Foil]] fencers with younger qualified fencers: in the top group the pause between [[Parry]] and [[Riposte]] was about half as long, and blade contact in the parry was shorter. See also [[Tactical Preparation]], and for how wheelchair fencing coaches rank the parts of preparation, [[Training Priorities in Wheelchair Fencing]].

## Tactical Preparation
short: Training that teaches a fencer how to prepare and choose actions for each situation of a bout.
source: Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing], textbook (1997), chapter "Tactical Preparation"; Gamaliy and Shevchuk, Technical-tactical preparation of fencers at different stages of long-term development (year not stated), pp. 152 to 155; Nikiforova, Markin and Udovichenko (2026), p. 138
status: library

Tyshler's textbook says tactical preparation covers how to prepare and apply a set of actions, how to carry out tactical intentions in real situations, tactical knowledge, and the mental processes behind decisions. Its main material is exercises close to bout conditions, together with tactical tasks carried into training bouts and competitions. Gamaliy and Shevchuk conclude that at top level a wrong decision is the main reason an action fails, so tactical preparation becomes the priority; they describe a computer tool for [[Video Analysis|bout analysis]] that models bouts against expected opponents. Nikiforova and colleagues add that the higher the level, the less pure technique separates fencers. See also [[Technical Preparation]].

## Physical Preparation
short: Training of the body for fencing: general work for all-round fitness and special work built from fencing actions.
source: Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing], textbook (1997), chapter "Physical Preparation"; Movshovich, Trenirovka yunykh fekhtovalschikov [Training Young Fencers] (2013)
status: library

Tyshler's textbook divides physical preparation into general and special. General physical preparation raises the body's working capacity and gives varied movement skills; the textbook favours sports such as basketball, football, tennis and boxing, whose demands resemble fencing. Special physical preparation develops the [[Physical Qualities]] through fencing actions themselves. Coaches should combine exercises that support each other and avoid those that harm a quality's use in fencing. Movshovich places all-round motor development first at ages 10 to 14 (see [[Long-Term Development Stages]]).

## Psychological Preparation
short: Training of the mental side of fencing: confidence, will, control of emotion and readiness for competition.
source: Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing], textbook (1997), chapters "Psychological Preparation", "Creating Readiness for Competition" and "Managing Performance in Competition"; Nikiforova, Markin and Udovichenko (2026), pp. 138 and 140
status: library

Tyshler's textbook defines psychological preparation as the means and methods that speed up learning to fence, strengthen mental qualities and will, and build confidence; its outcome is a state of readiness for [[Competition]]. Competitions shape it most, because each result changes how a fencer rates their own chances. During an event the coach guides the fencer through the warm-up [[Individual Lesson]], tactical advice before bouts and work on expectations. Nikiforova and colleagues list stress control, decisiveness, stability after a disputed [[Touch]] and the ability to close out the end of a bout. See also [[Psychological Strain]].

> I lost focus after that disputed touch.
> That is part of psychological preparation. We will rehearse it in scored bouts.

## Physical Qualities
short: An athlete's basic motor abilities: strength, speed, endurance, coordination (agility) and flexibility.
source: Zatsiorskiy, Fizicheskie kachestva sportsmena [Physical Qualities of the Athlete], 4th edition (2019), sections I.1.2 and IV.2.1; Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing], textbook (1997), chapter "Physical Preparation"
status: library

Zatsiorsky defines strength as the ability to produce a given amount of force through muscle effort, and flexibility as the ability to move through a large range of motion. Tyshler's textbook lists strength, speed, endurance, agility, flexibility and voluntary muscle relaxation for fencers. It ties strength in fencing to static and dynamic effort in the [[On Guard]] position and to control of the weapon, and stresses mobility of the shoulder of the [[Sword Arm]]. The other qualities have their own entries: [[Speed]], [[Endurance]] and [[Coordination Abilities]]; see also [[Muscle Relaxation]] and, for wheelchair fencing, [[Leading Physical Qualities]].

## Speed
short: The ability to make a movement in the shortest time the conditions allow, in a task too short for fatigue.
source: Zatsiorskiy, Fizicheskie kachestva sportsmena [Physical Qualities of the Athlete], 4th edition (2019), section II.1.1; Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing], textbook (1997), chapter "Physical Preparation"; Shustikov, Fedorov and Serdyukova, Initial training stage of children in sport fencing, Uchenye zapiski universiteta imeni P.F. Lesgafta (2019), no. 3 (169), p. 402
status: library

Zatsiorsky separates three forms of speed: reaction time, speed of a single movement and frequency of movement. They are largely independent, so a fencer can react quickly and still move slowly. Tyshler's textbook trains two sides in fencing: the speed of fencing movements, using actions at or near maximum speed, and reaction to a changing situation, using fencing signals (touch and sight for foil and épée, mainly sight for sabre). Shustikov and colleagues name speed of movement and of reaction among the qualities worth developing from age six. See [[Simple Reaction]] and [[Choice Reaction]].

## Endurance
short: The ability to resist fatigue and keep working without losing effectiveness.
source: Zatsiorskiy, Fizicheskie kachestva sportsmena [Physical Qualities of the Athlete], 4th edition (2019), section III.1.1; Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing], textbook (1997), chapter "Physical Preparation"; Movshovich, Trenirovka yunykh fekhtovalschikov [Training Young Fencers] (2013); Nikiforova, Markin and Udovichenko (2026), p. 140
status: library

Zatsiorsky defines endurance as the ability to resist fatigue, and fatigue as a temporary drop in working capacity caused by load. Tyshler's textbook notes that each hard [[Bout]] is followed by rest in which mental tension does not always settle, and that the deciding final bouts come when the fencer is most tired. Bouts are therefore the main means of building special endurance, with sports games and cyclic sports for general endurance. Movshovich warns that fatigue near the finals lowers activity and accuracy, and Nikiforova and colleagues train [[Recovery]] between bouts across a long competition day.

## Coordination Abilities
short: The ability to learn new movements quickly and to rebuild a movement when the situation changes; also called agility.
source: Zatsiorskiy, Fizicheskie kachestva sportsmena [Physical Qualities of the Athlete], 4th edition (2019), section IV.1.1; Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing], textbook (1997), chapter "Physical Preparation"; Movshovich, Trenirovka yunykh fekhtovalschikov [Training Young Fencers] (2013); Turdikulov, Main elements of the training system for young fencers, Fan-sportga (2025), no. 12, p. 63
status: library

Zatsiorsky's term is lovkost (agility): first the ability to master new movements, then the ability to change a movement quickly to fit a changing situation. Tyshler's textbook sees it in a fencer's ability to improvise against different opponents, and trains it with bouts against unfamiliar opponents, multi-action phrases, fencing from the opposite guard and changes of tempo and rhythm. Movshovich describes weapon-specific forms, such as getting the point through the opponent's defence at [[Foil]] and finding arm positions that gain time at [[Épée]]. Turdikulov lists coordination that makes movement precise in time, space and force among the selection criteria for young fencers.

## Simple Reaction
short: A known response to a known signal that appears suddenly, such as starting a set action on the coach's cue.
source: Zatsiorskiy, Fizicheskie kachestva sportsmena [Physical Qualities of the Athlete], 4th edition (2019), section II.2.1; Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing], textbook (1997), chapter "Initial Training"; Movshovich, Trenirovka yunykh fekhtovalschikov [Training Young Fencers] (2013)
status: library

Zatsiorsky measures reaction speed by the hidden (latent) time before a response, and defines a simple reaction as a known movement made to a known, suddenly appearing signal; every other reaction is complex. Tyshler's textbook links the simple reaction to pre-arranged actions and notes that it reaches its best values first as a young fencer grows, before the more complex reactions. Movshovich asks that work on speed of single movements and reactions, including the simple reaction, should lead in the first years of training at ages 10 to 14. Compare [[Choice Reaction]] and see [[Speed]] and [[Reaction Time and Movement Time]].

## Choice Reaction
short: Choosing the right response from several possible ones as the opponent acts, such as picking the parry that fits the attack.
source: Zatsiorskiy, Fizicheskie kachestva sportsmena [Physical Qualities of the Athlete], 4th edition (2019), section II.2; Movshovich, Trenirovka yunykh fekhtovalschikov [Training Young Fencers] (2013); Green, The Classical Foil Fencing Lesson (2016), p. 55
status: library

Zatsiorsky's own example of a choice reaction is a fencer choosing which [[Parry]] to take against the opponent's attack. Its time grows with uncertainty: the more varied the attacker's actions, the longer the defender needs. Reaction to a moving object is another complex reaction typical of combat sports; Movshovich links it (as perceptual anticipation) and the choice reaction to the success of actions at every age stage. Green describes classical choice reaction drills that are built up one option at a time. Compare [[Simple Reaction]] and [[Anticipation Reaction]].

> Why are we drilling two parries at once?
> Because in a bout you will have to choose the right one as I attack.

## Long-Term Development Stages
short: The stages a fencer passes through from first lessons to top results, each with its own aims and loads.
source: Saumova, Methods and organisation of the training process with young fencers, Evraziyskiy nauchnyy zhurnal (year not stated), pp. 7 to 8; Gamaliy and Shevchuk (year not stated), p. 147; Movshovich, Trenirovka yunykh fekhtovalschikov [Training Young Fencers] (2013); Turdikulov, Fan-sportga (2025), no. 12, pp. 62 to 64
status: library

Saumova sets out four stages for young fencers: preliminary preparation (from about age 9 or 10), initial specialisation, deepened specialisation and sports perfection; she notes that fencers usually reach stable results at the level of the Russian title master of sport at 18 to 20. Gamaliy and Shevchuk use five stages: initial preparation, preliminary basic, specialised basic, maximum use of individual potential, and keeping top results. Movshovich gives age bands of 10 to 14, 15 to 16 and 17 to 19, with individual technical and tactical work as the main task of the last band. Turdikulov warns against sudden jumps in load in adolescence and asks that loads fit the age while pointing towards top-level demands. See [[Initial Training Stage]], [[Training Load]] and [[Talent Identification]].

## Initial Training Stage
short: The first stage of fencing training, built on games, basic positions and a few simple actions.
source: Shustikov, Fedorov and Serdyukova, Initial training stage of children in sport fencing, Uchenye zapiski universiteta imeni P.F. Lesgafta (2019), no. 3 (169), pp. 401 to 404; Saumova, Evraziyskiy nauchnyy zhurnal (year not stated), pp. 8 to 9; Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing], textbook (1997), chapter "Initial Training"
status: library

Shustikov and colleagues found that children in St Petersburg and Moscow schools start at 6 or 7, although the programmes were written for age 8 and older. They make the game method the main method at this age and list game exercises for speed, reaction, coordination, spatial orientation and rhythm, with fencing equipment and with images (hold the grip like a bird, so it neither chokes nor flies away). Tyshler's textbook warns that adult-style exercises with many repetitions can hurt young children, and recommends basic positions, imitation exercises and a small set of attacks and defences. Saumova adds exercises with marks, mirror work with self-assessment and games in pairs. See [[Long-Term Development Stages]] and [[Paired Exercise]].

## Periodisation
short: Dividing the training year into periods and stages, each with a main aim, so the fencer peaks for the main competition.
source: Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing], textbook (1997), chapter "Training Structure and Functional Preparation"; Tyshler and Ryzhkova, Fekhtovanie. Tekhniko-takticheskaya i funktsionalnaya trenirovka [Fencing: Technical-Tactical and Functional Training] (2010), pp. 39 to 43; Nikiforova, Markin and Udovichenko (2026), pp. 139 to 142
status: library

Tyshler's textbook names four training cycles: the [[Microcycle]] (usually a week), the mesocycle (a stage or period), the macrocycle (a year) and the megacycle (four years); its model year has a preparatory, a main and a transition period. Tyshler and Ryzhkova instead divide the year into a base period, a forming period and a post-competition period, and say periodisation makes sense from about age 15 or 16. Both stress that the division is only approximate, because qualified fencers enter 15 to 25 competitions a year. Nikiforova and colleagues describe a year with two peaks for the Russian national épée squad, in March to April and June to August. See [[Peaking for the Main Competition]].

## Microcycle
short: A short training cycle, usually one week, that sets the order of loads and rest days.
source: Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing], textbook (1997), chapter "Training Structure and Functional Preparation"; Tyshler and Ryzhkova (2010), pp. 43 to 44
status: library

For a training camp, Tyshler's textbook proposes five days of special training in blocks of two and three days, with a physical training day between them and light work on the seventh day; this mirrors two-day and three-day championships. Near competitions it names three kinds: pre-competition, competition and post-competition microcycles, which raise, hold or lower the load. The more experienced the fencer, the more often the direction of the microcycle should change. Tyshler and Ryzhkova note that club fencers usually train four days a week, two and two, with a rest day midweek. See [[Periodisation]] and [[Training Load]].

## Peaking for the Main Competition
short: Bringing a fencer to best form at the most important competition of the season.
source: Nikiforova, Markin and Udovichenko, Preparing qualified fencers for the main competitions of the season, Uchenye zapiski universiteta imeni P.F. Lesgafta (2026), no. 2 (252), pp. 137 to 143; Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing], textbook (1997), chapter "Organisational and Methodical Bases"
status: library

Tyshler's textbook calls the target state sport form (sportivnaya forma): competitive readiness that lets a fencer show their full trained level. Nikiforova and colleagues, observing 12 Russian national épée fencers, name eight planning factors for the last stage before the main event, led by technical and tactical work (29.8 percent), physical and functional readiness (20.3 percent) and readiness to fence in competition mode (13.3 percent). In their seven-stage year, a stage of mental unloading follows the spring peak, two days of scored bouting enter each week before the main event, and [[Psychological Strain]] rises step by step. They cite findings that one or two weeks of low mental strain after a high level of training cost the fencer stability and accuracy of reactions. See [[Periodisation]].

## Training Load
short: The total demand of training on a fencer, described by volume, intensity and psychological strain.
source: Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing], textbook (1997), chapter "Organisational and Methodical Bases"; Tyshler and Ryzhkova (2010), pp. 29 to 30; Nikiforova, Markin and Udovichenko (2026), pp. 140 and 143; Turdikulov, Fan-sportga (2025), no. 12, p. 63
status: library

Tyshler's textbook says that counting only the physical side of load is not enough in fencing: emotion, will and sensory strain form a psychological part of the load that also shapes how the body responds. Tyshler and Ryzhkova report heart-rate totals of 12,000 to 18,000 beats in one session for qualified fencers, mainly because of mental strain. Nikiforova and colleagues treat motor intensity and [[Psychological Strain]] as linked parts of intensity. Turdikulov names gradual increase, wave-like changes and cycles as the general principles of load for young fencers. See also [[Recovery]].

## Psychological Strain
short: The mental load of a training task or bout, rated from solo drills (lowest) to bouts in major competitions (highest).
source: Ryzhkova and Kravtsov, Distributing types of specialised activity by psychological strain, Teoriya i praktika fizicheskoy kultury (2021), no. 12, pp. 25 to 27; Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing], textbook (1997), figure 13; Ryzhkova, Petrenko, Pakhomova and Nikolaeva (2023), no. 5, pp. 31 to 32
status: library

Soviet coaching literature calls it psikhicheskaya napryazhennost. Ryzhkova and Kravtsov refine the older Keller and Saichuk scale into ten points: solo movement drills 1, target work or shadow bouts 2, pre-arranged paired exercises 3, paired exercises with choice 4, free paired exercises 5, [[Individual Lesson]] 6, training bouts with a coach's task 7, scored training bouts 8, competitions that do not affect the fencer's ranking 9, and ranking competitions 10. They advise that the strain of a training session should not exceed competition level, so the fencer reaches the main event at peak form. Ryzhkova and colleagues measured skin conductance in four foil fencers and found a stronger response after 15-touch bouts in competition than after the same bouts in training. See [[Training Bout]] and [[Solo Exercise]].

> Why is the lesson before the scored bouts and not after?
> We plan the mental load of the whole session, not only the physical work.

## Recovery
short: Restoring working capacity after training and competition, through rest, a change of activity and planned easier days.
source: Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing], textbook (1997), chapter "Means and Methods of Recovery"; Tyshler and Ryzhkova (2010), pp. 41 to 44; Nikiforova, Markin and Udovichenko (2026), p. 141
status: library

Tyshler's textbook notes that fencing loads both body and mind: the weight of weapon and kit, poor heat loss in closed clothing, strain on sight, and quick swings between good and bad results. It lists overload of the elbow and shoulder of the [[Sword Arm]] as typical for qualified fencers. Its pedagogical means of recovery rely on switching to other activities and sports for mental relief.
