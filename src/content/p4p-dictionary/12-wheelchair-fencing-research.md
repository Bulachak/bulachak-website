## Wheelchair Fencing Research
short: The scientific study of wheelchair fencers. A 2026 review found it makes up a very small share of all fencing research.
source: Holmes K., Bottoms L., Experimental and quantitative observational research in fencing and wheelchair fencing: A scoping review (2026), PLoS One 21(6), Results and Discussion
status: library

A scoping review of 445 experimental and quantitative studies of fencers found that 3.4 percent studied wheelchair fencers and 0.9 percent studied wheelchair and standing fencers together. Across fencing research as a whole, most studies had fewer than 50 participants, and laboratory and cross-sectional designs were the most common. The authors call for research built for para athletes in biomechanics, physiology, performance, training, injury prevention and rehabilitation. Many entries in this group rest on one study or one research team, so each one reports what was found in the fencers studied, not a rule for all of [[Parafencing]].

## Postural Muscle Activation
short: The early work of the abdominal and back muscles that steadies the trunk when a fencer attacks from the chair.
source: Borysiuk Z., Błaszczyszyn M., Piechota K., Nowicki T., Movement Patterns of Polish National Paralympic Team Wheelchair Fencers with Regard to Muscle Activity and Co-Activation Time (2022), Journal of Human Kinetics 82, pp. 223 to 232
status: library

In an EMG study of 16 right-handed fencers of the Polish Paralympic team, the abdominal and back muscles were activated early when fencers thrust at a visual signal, at the same time as the triceps of the [[Sword Arm]]. The authors read this as a centrally programmed anticipatory mechanism that stabilises the technical action, and they link it to the APA (anticipatory postural adjustment) phenomenon. The study split the fencers into two groups by level of impairment: group A (7 fencers) and group B (9 fencers). In group B, the back and abdominal pair on the left side, opposite the sword arm, showed the higher [[Muscle Co-Activation]], which the authors interpret as the postural base for attacks in that group. Compare [[Category A]] and [[Category B]], and see [[Trunk Muscle Training]].

> Why are we working on my back and stomach muscles for my attack?
> In the Polish study those muscles started working at the same moment as the arm.

## Muscle Co-Activation
short: Two opposing muscles working at the same moment around a joint. Researchers express it as a co-activation index, in percent.
source: Borysiuk Z., Błaszczyszyn M., Piechota K., Nowicki T., Movement Patterns of Polish National Paralympic Team Wheelchair Fencers with Regard to Muscle Activity and Co-Activation Time (2022), Journal of Human Kinetics 82, pp. 223 to 232
status: library

Co-activation is the share of a movement during which an agonist muscle and its antagonist are both active, measured with [[Surface Electromyography]]. It helps stabilise joints and control movement. In the Polish Paralympic team study, the index for key muscle pairs (upper arm, forearm, and back with abdomen) during thrusts ranged from about 48 to 51 percent, and the authors propose this range as a standard for wheelchair fencing. They add that a larger sample is needed before the result can be generalised. Group A showed higher co-activation of the forearm muscle pair, which the authors connect to accuracy in hitting the target. See [[Postural Muscle Activation]].

## Movement Pattern
short: The order and timing in which muscles switch on during a fencing action, as recorded by EMG.
source: Borysiuk Z., Błaszczyszyn M., Piechota K., Nowicki T., Movement Patterns of Polish National Paralympic Team Wheelchair Fencers with Regard to Muscle Activity and Co-Activation Time (2022), Journal of Human Kinetics 82, pp. 223 to 232
status: library

Researchers judge whether a movement pattern is correct mainly from the sequence in which muscle groups activate in response to a signal. In the Polish study, the fencers in group B changed their pattern more from one attempt to the next, while group A fencers kept a similar activation pattern at relatively low tension. Group A also had shorter activation times in almost every muscle tested. The authors suggest that years of technical learning reshape motor control, raising the co-activation of postural and forearm muscles (see [[Muscle Co-Activation]]). The actions studied were thrusts of the kind used in an [[Attack]].

## Surface Electromyography
short: Recording muscle activity through electrodes on the skin, to see which muscles work, when, and how strongly. Usually shortened to sEMG.
source: Borysiuk Z., Błaszczyszyn M., Piechota K., Nowicki T., Movement Patterns of Polish National Paralympic Team Wheelchair Fencers (2022), Journal of Human Kinetics 82, pp. 224 to 225; Borysiuk Z., Piechota K., Minkiewicz T., Analysis of performance of the fencing lunge with regard to the difficulty level of a technical-tactical task (2013), Journal of Combat Sports and Martial Arts 4, pp. 135 to 139
status: library

The Polish wheelchair study used a 16-channel system with electrodes on the deltoid, triceps and biceps of the [[Sword Arm]], two forearm muscles, and the external oblique and latissimus dorsi on both sides of the trunk. An earlier study of a standing épée fencer combined sEMG with synchronised high-speed cameras. Its authors describe EMG and high-speed cameras as the best tools for analysing an athlete's motor patterns, and suggest using them on larger groups of athletes. Both are laboratory methods, unlike [[Video Analysis]] of competition bouts. See [[Movement Pattern]].

## Thrust to a Visual Stimulus
short: A test task: the fencer thrusts at the coach's trunk as soon as the coach's blade moves. Used in EMG studies of wheelchair fencers.
source: Borysiuk Z., Błaszczyszyn M., Piechota K., Nowicki T., Movement Patterns of Polish National Paralympic Team Wheelchair Fencers with Regard to Muscle Activity and Co-Activation Time (2022), Journal of Human Kinetics 82, p. 225
status: library

In the Polish study, the fencer's wheelchair was set on a fencing platform after a 20 to 25 minute individual warm-up. The distance was measured from the tip of the fencer's weapon to the coach's bent elbow. The coach moved the blade from the quarte [[Parry]] position to sixte, and the fencer answered with a direct thrust, while an accelerometer on the coach's [[Guard]] marked the signal. Each set held three attempts. Compare the competition measurement in [[Fencing Distance]].

> Where do I set the distance for this test?
> From the tip of your weapon to my bent elbow, as the Polish study did.

## Trunk Muscle Training
short: Exercises that strengthen the back, abdominal and shoulder girdle muscles, often done seated in the fencing wheelchair.
source: Borysiuk Z. et al., Movement Patterns of Polish National Paralympic Team Wheelchair Fencers (2022), Journal of Human Kinetics 82, Practical implications; Gorokhova N.P., Rubtsova N.O., Dinamika fizicheskoy podgotovlennosti podrostkov s narusheniem funktsiy oporno-dvigatelnogo apparata v protsesse zanyatiy fekhtovaniem na kolyaskakh [Changes in the physical preparedness of adolescents with musculoskeletal impairment in wheelchair fencing] (2019), Uchenye zapiski universiteta imeni P.F. Lesgafta 3(169); Yulamanova G.M., Emelyanov E.I., Rumyantseva E.R., Issledovanie znachimosti vidov sportivnoy podgotovki (na primere fekhtovaniya na kolyaskakh) [The importance of the types of preparation: wheelchair fencing] (2009), Uchenye zapiski 7(53), p. 115
status: library

The Polish EMG authors recommend adding exercises for the postural (back and abdominal) muscles to explosive strength training in wheelchair fencing, expecting better coordination and speed. In a Russian program for adolescents with cerebral palsy, exercises for the back, abdomen and upper shoulder girdle were done from several starting positions, mostly seated in the [[Fencing Wheelchair]] without leaning on the [[Backrest]]. Yulamanova and colleagues describe one aim of physical preparation as strengthening the muscular corset (myshechnyy korset). See [[Postural Muscle Activation]].

> Can I do these against the backrest?
> In that program they were done mostly seated without back support.

## Trunk Lean
short: Bending the trunk forward, back or to the side while seated. Russian sources pair it with the lunge as a way to reach a deeper distance.
source: Yulamanova G.M., Emelyanov E.I., Rumyantseva E.R., Issledovanie znachimosti vidov sportivnoy podgotovki (na primere fekhtovaniya na kolyaskakh) (2009), Uchenye zapiski 7(53), p. 117; Dubkova E.S., Voynova E.V., Gorokhova N.P., Tekhnologiya organizatsii samostoyatelnykh zanyatiy [Organising self-directed sessions] (2020), Uchenye zapiski universiteta imeni P.F. Lesgafta 4(182), pp. 149 to 150; Gorokhova N.P., Usloviya pedagogicheskogo soprovozhdeniya protsessa individualizatsii [Conditions for supporting individualisation] (2020), Azimuth of Scientific Research: Pedagogy and Psychology 9(2)
status: library

The Russian sources call it uklon or naklon tulovishcha. Yulamanova and colleagues list attacks and parries performed with a lunge or a trunk lean, as the depth of the distance grows, among the actions that show speed-strength. A home training plan by Dubkova and colleagues asks for thrusts at a target with one arm and no forward lean, and uses leans to the opposite side and backward, within each fencer's range, as a break between weapon exercises. Gorokhova reports that in some young fencers with spastic diplegia a reflex threw the trunk sharply backward; attacking from there was not practical, so the coach built a reply with an action on the opponent's blade instead. In competition the seat sets the limit: see [[Lifting from the Seat]] and [[Losing Balance]].

> Should I lean in more when I hit the target at home?
> Not on this drill. The plan asks for thrusts with one arm and no forward lean.

## Action Complexity and Timing
short: In one épée study, harder technical and tactical tasks took longer to perform and needed more muscle activity.
source: Borysiuk Z., Piechota K., Minkiewicz T., Analysis of performance of the fencing lunge with regard to the difficulty level of a technical-tactical task (2013), Journal of Combat Sports and Martial Arts 4, pp. 135 to 139
status: library

This case study filmed and recorded one 22-year-old female épée fencer of the Polish junior team. A lunge on her own initiative took 0.64 s on average, a lunge when the opponent broke blade contact 0.67 s, and a lunge with a disengagement of the opponent's [[Parry]] 0.81 s. Peak muscle activity was highest in the most complex task, and in most trials the first part of the action was the movement of the [[Sword Arm]]. The study measured the leg muscles of a standing fencer; the sources in this group do not test the same tasks from the wheelchair.

## Reaction Time and Movement Time
short: Reaction time runs from an unexpected signal to the start of the response. Movement time runs from the first muscle activity to the end of the movement.
source: Borysiuk Z., Cynarski W.J., Psychomotor aspects of talent identification: A new approach in the case of fencing (2010), Archives of Budo 6(2), pp. 91 to 94; Yulamanova G.M., Emelyanov E.I., Rumyantseva E.R., Issledovanie znachimosti vidov sportivnoy podgotovki (na primere fekhtovaniya na kolyaskakh) (2009), Uchenye zapiski 7(53), p. 116
status: library

Borysiuk and Cynarski measure three kinds of reaction: simple, choice and spatial anticipation (see [[Anticipation Reaction]]). In their study of 22 novice and 16 advanced standing fencers, the advanced fencers had shorter simple and choice reaction times, and most of the gain was in the middle phase: they perceived and decided faster. In a survey of wheelchair fencing coaches and athletes, speed abilities, including reactivity and speed of decision, ranked first among physical qualities (see [[Leading Physical Qualities]]). The Polish wheelchair EMG study measured a related response with the [[Thrust to a Visual Stimulus]] task.

## Anticipation Reaction
short: Responding at the right moment to a signal the fencer can foresee, such as the start of an opponent's move. Reacting too early counts as an error.
source: Borysiuk Z., Cynarski W.J., Psychomotor aspects of talent identification: A new approach in the case of fencing (2010), Archives of Budo 6(2), pp. 92 to 93
status: library

The authors define it as anticipating which stimulus will occur, or the response to it, and also call it event anticipation. In their tests, advanced fencers reacted to spatial anticipation signals significantly more effectively than novices: they processed the signals very early and responded at the proper time. Responses that came too fast were recorded as incorrect. The tests were done with standing fencers. See [[Reaction Time and Movement Time]] and [[Talent Identification]].

## Talent Identification
short: Recognising which young fencers are likely to reach a high level. One review argues that psychomotor factors predict it better than body build.
source: Borysiuk Z., Cynarski W.J., Psychomotor aspects of talent identification: A new approach in the case of fencing (2010), Archives of Budo 6(2), pp. 91 to 94
status: library

Borysiuk and Cynarski draw on studies of standing fencers, including 127 members of the Polish national team at several training levels. They report that body build had limited influence on results and mostly sorted fencers between weapons, and that VO2 max did not separate champions from average fencers. Choice reaction time and spatial anticipation are named as the main predictors (see [[Anticipation Reaction]]). They define talent as the ability to acquire motor programs effectively and to carry them out in combat, and they argue that selection should continue as development from beginner to championship level. None of the fencers studied competed in a [[Fencing Wheelchair]].

## Individualisation
short: Building training around each fencer's own abilities and impairment, not around a group. Russian adaptive-sport literature treats it as the fullest form of differentiation.
source: Gorokhova N.P., Usloviya pedagogicheskogo soprovozhdeniya protsessa individualizatsii na zanyatiyakh s yunymi fekhtovalshchikami na kolyaskakh [Conditions for supporting individualisation in classes with young wheelchair fencers] (2020), Azimuth of Scientific Research: Pedagogy and Psychology 9(2)
status: library

Gorokhova describes the process for fencers aged 14 to 16 with cerebral palsy at a Moscow boarding school. Before training, the coach records the main condition, its form and degree, and any additional or accompanying conditions, and chooses means and methods to suit. Physical preparation followed each fencer's movement limits, such as joint contractures, muscle tone and spasticity, and their tolerance of load. Fencing actions were taught only in a third stage, after corrective work on habitual postures and the forming of a new motor habit. The author holds that teaching these individual actions, and correcting their errors, is possible only in an individual lesson with the coach. See [[Working Posture]] and, for the separate competition process, [[Classification]].

> Why can't she learn the parry with the rest of the group yet?
> In this approach, posture work comes first. The fencing actions come in the third stage.

## Working Posture
short: Each fencer's best position with the weapon in the wheelchair: the one that lets them fence with the least effort. Russian sources call it rabochaya poza fekhtovalshchika.
source: Gorokhova N.P., Usloviya pedagogicheskogo soprovozhdeniya protsessa individualizatsii na zanyatiyakh s yunymi fekhtovalshchikami na kolyaskakh (2020), Azimuth of Scientific Research: Pedagogy and Psychology 9(2)
status: library

Gorokhova describes individual technical aids, made to correct habitual postures and to find a specialised position with the weapon in the [[Fencing Wheelchair]]. How the weapon is held depends on fine motor control and the strength of the hand. With beginners who had marked spastic weakness of the arms, the coach bound the handle to the hand with an elastic bandage, wound loosely, so the young fencer could move with less muscle tension. This was a training practice at the start stage; competition has its own rules on [[Strapping]] and handles ([[Weapon Dimensions]]), and in [[Category C]] the weapon is fixed to the hand. See [[Individualisation]].

## Weapon Choice in Adaptive Training
short: Matching a beginner's weapon, grip and hand to how they move. One Russian study linked foil and épée to different movement profiles.
source: Gorokhova N.P., Usloviya pedagogicheskogo soprovozhdeniya protsessa individualizatsii na zanyatiyakh s yunymi fekhtovalshchikami na kolyaskakh (2020), Azimuth of Scientific Research: Pedagogy and Psychology 9(2)
status: library

In Gorokhova's group of young fencers with cerebral palsy, the choice of weapon ([[Épée]] or [[Foil]]), grip (French or pistol) and hand (left or right) depended on the degree of motor impairment. Foil, described as fast and demanding complex coordination and anticipation, suited learners who already moved the arm and hand in isolation. Épée suited learners with spastic-type high muscle tone, because it let them fence under more static load. This is one coach-researcher's observation for one group, not a rule of [[Sport Class]].

> Should he start with foil or épée?
> In that study the choice depended on how each young fencer moved, so it varied by fencer.

## Leading Physical Qualities
short: The physical abilities that coaches and athletes rank highest for wheelchair fencing. In one survey: speed, then coordination, then endurance.
source: Yulamanova G.M., Emelyanov E.I., Rumyantseva E.R., Issledovanie znachimosti vidov sportivnoy podgotovki dlya sportsmenov s porazheniem oporno-dvigatelnogo apparata (na primere fekhtovaniya na kolyaskakh) [The importance of the types of preparation for athletes with musculoskeletal impairment: wheelchair fencing] (2009), Uchenye zapiski 7(53), pp. 116 to 118
status: library

The survey covered 11 leading coaches and 20 wheelchair fencers from Russia, Ukraine and Belarus. Both groups ranked speed abilities first, coordination second and endurance third. Endurance was tied to staying sharp across a competition day of several [[Pool]] bouts and at least one [[Direct Elimination]] bout, and coordination to the accuracy of the final thrust, which depends on control of the trunk and shoulder girdle muscles and above all the hand of the [[Sword Arm]]. Every respondent rated maximal weights as ineffective, since strength plays a supporting role, and most favoured exercises at maximal intensity that model the bout. On minimal rest intervals they split: over 63 percent of coaches favoured them, while 75 percent of athletes found them unacceptable.

> Should I be lifting heavy this block?
> In that survey, every coach and athlete rated maximal weights as ineffective for wheelchair fencing.

## Training Priorities in Wheelchair Fencing
short: How coaches and athletes in one survey ranked the parts of preparation: technical first, physical second.
source: Yulamanova G.M., Emelyanov E.I., Rumyantseva E.R., Issledovanie znachimosti vidov sportivnoy podgotovki (na primere fekhtovaniya na kolyaskakh) (2009), Uchenye zapiski 7(53), pp. 115 to 116
status: library

In the same survey, both coaches and athletes put technical preparation first and physical preparation second. Coaches ranked psychological preparation third and tactical preparation fourth; athletes put tactical third. The coaches explained the low tactical rank by saying that, compared with standing fencing, wheelchair fencing uses multi-tempo combinations less often, relies on positional preparation, and builds most of a [[Bout]] on conditioned actions that rest on simple reactions. The authors conclude that tactical and psychological preparation cannot be separated, and that physical preparation is the foundation for technique (see [[Leading Physical Qualities]]).

## Muscle Relaxation
short: Letting muscles that are not needed switch off, so the working ones are not overloaded. Russian sources stress it for wheelchair fencers.
source: Yulamanova G.M., Emelyanov E.I., Rumyantseva E.R., Issledovanie znachimosti vidov sportivnoy podgotovki (na primere fekhtovaniya na kolyaskakh) (2009), Uchenye zapiski 7(53), p. 115; Gorokhova N.P., Usloviya pedagogicheskogo soprovozhdeniya protsessa individualizatsii (2020), Azimuth of Scientific Research: Pedagogy and Psychology 9(2); Dubkova E.S., Voynova E.V., Gorokhova N.P., Tekhnologiya organizatsii samostoyatelnykh zanyatiy (2020), Uchenye zapiski universiteta imeni P.F. Lesgafta 4(182), pp. 149 to 150
status: library

Yulamanova and colleagues write that physical preparation should teach fencers to relax antagonist muscles that take no part in the action, which lowers the load on the working muscles and raises the volume of work. They add that the tone of the shoulder muscles should not dominate the tone of the forearm and hand of the [[Sword Arm]]. Gorokhova used shaking of the limbs, seated or lying on a mat, in the rest intervals to release tension. A home session plan by Dubkova and colleagues ends with breathing, relaxation and stretching exercises. Compare [[Muscle Co-Activation]], where opposing muscles work together on purpose.

## Fitness Changes from Wheelchair Fencing
short: What changed in teenagers with cerebral palsy who added a wheelchair fencing program to school physical education, in one controlled Russian study.
source: Gorokhova N.P., Rubtsova N.O., Dinamika fizicheskoy podgotovlennosti podrostkov s narusheniem funktsiy oporno-dvigatelnogo apparata v protsesse zanyatiy fekhtovaniem na kolyaskakh (2019), Uchenye zapiski universiteta imeni P.F. Lesgafta 3(169), tables 1 to 3
status: library

The study compared 10 adolescents with spastic diplegia who joined the fencing program with 10 classmates who did not. Tests were done seated in the chair: grip strength, a 1 kg medicine-ball throw, ball catching and throwing, and hitting a swinging ball with a stick. After the program the fencing group had a lower resting breathing rate (15.3 against 18.8 per minute), a greater vital capacity, a better Ruffier index of cardiovascular reserve, and caught on average 7.8 balls out of 10 against 4.2. Grip strength and throws also improved, while habitual positions of the trunk and legs remained. The program included [[Trunk Muscle Training]] in the [[Fencing Wheelchair]], and the study is small and from one school.

## Neuromuscular Changes from Wheelchair Fencing
short: Changes in reaction, rhythm and spatial orientation in young fencers with cerebral palsy, measured in one Russian study against a control group.
source: Gorokhova N.P., Vliyanie zanyatiy fekhtovaniem na kolyaskakh na izmeneniya v nervno-myshechnom apparate u podrostkov s detskim tserebralnym paralichom [The effect of wheelchair fencing on the neuromuscular system of adolescents with cerebral palsy] (2021), Physical Culture. Sport. Tourism. Motor Recreation 6(2)
status: library

Ten fencers aged 12 to 14, members of the Moscow para fencing team, trained 60 minutes twice a week, and 10 pupils from the same school who played active games formed the control group. All tests started seated in the wheelchair: catching a ball thrown from 3 m, throwing a tennis ball at a 25 cm target 3 m away, tapping in time to a metronome, and pushing the chair 10 m in a straight line with eyes closed. The fencing group improved significantly in the catching and throwing tests compared with the control group, and their results became more uniform. The author links this to better coordination of the hand flexors and extensors and of spatial and timing sense. Compare the separate fitness results in [[Fitness Changes from Wheelchair Fencing]] and the reaction measures in [[Reaction Time and Movement Time]].

## Self-Directed Training
short: Sessions a fencer does alone, usually at home, from a plan the coach sends. Used when a fencer cannot reach the training hall.
source: Dubkova E.S., Voynova E.V., Gorokhova N.P., Tekhnologiya organizatsii samostoyatelnykh zanyatiy, kak dopolnitelnaya forma sportivnoy podgotovki fekhtovalshchikov s porazheniem oporno-dvigatelnogo apparata [Organising self-directed sessions as an additional form of training for fencers with musculoskeletal impairment] (2020), Uchenye zapiski universiteta imeni P.F. Lesgafta 4(182), pp. 146 to 151
status: library

The authors describe home sessions for experienced wheelchair fencers in Moscow during the 2020 hall closures, and for the times each year when athletes attend rehabilitation courses. The coach emails a session plan; the athlete keeps a sports diary of exercises, repetitions and how they felt, records heart rate at the start and end, and sends the data to the coach daily so the load can be adjusted. The sample plan needs a 1.5 by 1.5 m space for an everyday chair with the brakes on, a cushion as a target, a foil or épée, light dumbbells, an elastic band and a computer for [[Video Analysis]]. The main part has thrusts at targets of 20, 10 and 5 cm, imitated parries in four and six with direct and disengagement [[Riposte|ripostes]], movement drills from [[On Guard]] at short and medium distance, and ideomotor (mental rehearsal) training in the chair. The authors note that partner work and the individual lesson cannot happen at home, and compensate with more general physical work and with accuracy and the sense of distance on the target.

> I can't get to the club this month. Do I just rest?
> No. I will send you a session plan, and you keep the diary and send me your heart rate each day.

## Communication Skills Development
short: Coaching that helps young fencers with musculoskeletal impairment express themselves, cooperate and settle disagreements in a team.
source: Gorokhova N.P., Tekhnologii lichnostno-orientirovannogo pedagogicheskogo protsessa razvitiya kommunikativnykh sposobnostey u yunykh fekhtovalshchikov s porazheniem oporno-dvigatelnogo apparata [Person-centred methods for developing communication skills in young fencers with musculoskeletal impairment] (2022), Uchenye zapiski universiteta imeni P.F. Lesgafta 2(204), pp. 98 to 102
status: library

Gorokhova worked with wheelchair fencers aged 14 to 16 at a Moscow sports centre, using observation, parent surveys and individual and group talks. The approach is person-centred: it builds on each athlete's own experience and pairs the teaching of fencing actions with spoken discussion, in the individual lesson, of a chosen simple or compound action such as an [[Attack]]. The author observed that the fencers expressed their thoughts more freely, judged their own defeats less harshly, showed empathy toward a losing partner, grew more interested in competitions and solved more problems without help. These findings come from observation, not from measured tests.

## Paralympic History of Wheelchair Fencing
short: Wheelchair fencing has been on the program of every Paralympic Games since the first, in Rome in 1960. Frames to hold the chairs came in during the 1950s.
source: Holmes K., Bottoms L., Experimental and quantitative observational research in fencing and wheelchair fencing: A scoping review (2026), PLoS One 21(6), Introduction; Mishra H., Wheelchair Fencing (student project, undated), History of Wheelchair Fencing
status: library

The scoping review notes that wheelchair fencing has been contested at every Paralympic Games since Rome 1960. A student project summary gives the early story: the sport was shown as a demonstration at the 1954 International Stoke Mandeville Games, sabre competition was added the next year, a women's [[Foil]] event followed in 1956, and [[Épée]] the year after. Fencers first used heavy chairs, and as lighter chairs moved during bouts, a helper would crouch behind each fencer to hold the chair. By the 1957 Stoke Mandeville Games frames were being tried, and they were developed further in the Netherlands and then Italy into the type of [[Frame]] used today (see [[Wheelchair Fixing]]). At Rome 1960 the same source lists three medal events, men's [[Sabre]], men's sabre [[Team Event|team]] and women's foil, all contested by Italian fencers.

## Fencing Injury Research
short: Studies of how fencers get hurt. A 2026 review found them scarce, with few on the weapon arm and shoulder and very few on wheelchair fencers.
source: Holmes K., Bottoms L., Experimental and quantitative observational research in fencing and wheelchair fencing: A scoping review (2026), PLoS One 21(6), Results and Discussion; Borysiuk Z. et al., Movement Patterns of Polish National Paralympic Team Wheelchair Fencers (2022), Journal of Human Kinetics 82, Discussion
status: library

In the review, injury and epidemiology made up 7.9 percent of fencing studies, recovery and rehabilitation 4.3 percent, and training load and fatigue 1.6 percent. The authors note that fewer studies have examined upper-limb injuries, despite the demands on the weapon arm and shoulder, and that short or cross-sectional studies may capture sudden injuries better than overuse. They call for long-term injury tracking, load monitoring and para-specific rehabilitation research. The Polish EMG authors interpret the longer muscle activation they saw in group B as a stabilising function that protects against injury, which is their reading, not a measured injury result (see [[Postural Muscle Activation]]). The review gives no injury rates for wheelchair fencers; see [[Wheelchair Fencing Research]] and [[Sword Arm]].
