## Coach Position at the Frame
short: Where a standing coach places feet, body and guard when giving a lesson to a fencer fixed in the frame.
source: IWAS Wheelchair Fencing Coaching Video 1: Introduction, Baldip Sahota and Jacques Portal, World Para Fencing coaching series, 01:32 to 01:45 and 06:37 to 07:39
status: library

Sahota's first video shows the coach standing beside the [[Frame]], with the front toe in line with a line marked on the frame, so the lesson keeps the same line as a bout. The two then check the working distance by reaching with the arm, much as fencers do when they set the [[Fencing Distance]]. Because the seated fencer's guard sits lower than a standing coach's, Sahota has the coach drop to about a quarter lunge, so that both guards are at the same height and the points are up. Only then do the exercises begin. The same video points out the main things a standing coach must judge: where to stand, how to hold the body and how to read distance from above. See [[Individual Lesson]].

## Changing Distance in the Lesson
short: The coach moves closer and further during a seated lesson so the fencer never drills against a still target.
source: Sabre Wheelchair Fencing Coaching: Demonstrating Advanced Warm-up Drills, World Abilitysport coaching series, 00:00 to 00:14 and 01:17 to 01:34; Maestro Gary M. van der Wege, PARA-COACHING: Adapting Able-Body Training Techniques to the Chair, USFCA and Team USA para fencing curriculum, 24:17 to 24:29 and 35:48 to 36:00
status: library

The fencer's chair is fixed, so in a lesson the changes of distance have to come from the coach. In the sabre warm-up video the coach sits loosely rather than properly in the chair, because that makes it easier to move back and forth and change distance often without warning the fencer. Van der Wege makes the same point: the coach should be moving too, since drilling against a stationary opponent is not realistic. He adds that, as in standing fencing, fencers should know their own lunge distance and use small but constant changes of distance. See [[Fencing Distance]], [[Seated Footwork]] and [[Individual Lesson]].

> Why do you keep sliding back between cuts?
> So you learn to find the distance yourself. In a bout nobody stays still for you.

## Seated Footwork
short: Moving forward and back in a fixed chair by leaning the trunk, in place of steps.
source: IWAS Wheelchair Fencing Coaching Video 1: Introduction, Baldip Sahota and Jacques Portal, 00:43 to 01:07 and 04:25; Maestro Gary M. van der Wege, PARA-COACHING, 19:29 to 19:55, 22:23 to 22:34 and 37:33 to 38:00
status: library

Van der Wege's phrase is that chair fencing "has footwork too". The distance is controlled by leaning the torso, so sitting bolt upright is not the aim once the bout starts. The guard position, sitting centred in the chair, is the starting point. In a lesson he treats a slight lean forward as the advance, full forward extension as the lunge, and a lean back as a half retreat, with the fencer leaning as far back as possible for a full retreat. Sahota describes the same equivalents and notes that the hips twist as the fencer leans back. In sabre, van der Wege says the advance or retreat must be planned before the command, as there is no time to react to the opponent's first move. See [[Trunk Lean]], [[Seated Lunge]] and [[Full Retreat]].

## Seated Lunge
short: The full forward extension from the chair, the seated form of the lunge, taught with set checkpoints.
source: IWAS Wheelchair Fencing Coaching Video 2: Four Simple Actions, Baldip Sahota and Jacques Portal, 00:52 to 01:21 and 06:34 to 06:49; IWAS Wheelchair Fencing Coaching Video 1: Introduction, 01:07 to 01:20, 04:25 and 08:12 to 08:50
status: library

In Sahota's videos the coach looks for the same points every time: the arm straight at the end, the guard above the shoulder, the point level with the target, the body leaning at an angle towards the hit, and the back hand holding the wheelchair or frame so the fencer does not fall over. Next to the plain lunge, the first video shows a lean forward lunge, the chair version of a step forward and lunge, and a lean back lunge, the version of stepping back and then lunging. A [[Category B]] fencer pushes with the back arm to lunge and pulls with it to return. See [[Back Hand Hold]], [[Seated Footwork]] and [[Lifting from the Seat]].

## Full Retreat
short: Leaning back as far as the body allows to take the target out of the opponent's reach.
source: Maestro Gary M. van der Wege, PARA-COACHING, 13:40 to 13:54, 22:34 to 22:48, 37:23 to 37:33, 38:29 to 38:58 and 39:24 to 39:51
status: library

Van der Wege notes that many high level fencers have developed great flexibility and can bend far back over the chair; in [[Sabre]] some place the torso almost parallel to the floor. He says fencers usually prefer the lowest allowed back and side guard because it gives the most room for this movement. In sabre he asks coaches to stress, in lessons, a retreat that also pulls the arm back far enough to protect it, with the knuckles turned out and the hand low enough not to open the flank. He also describes a defensive guard in the retreat, with the blade held across the middle of the body. See [[Seated Footwork]], [[Backrest]] and [[Losing Balance]].

## Back Hand Hold
short: The non-sword hand holds the wheelchair, frame or a handle, for balance and, for some fencers, to move.
source: IWAS Wheelchair Fencing Coaching Video 1: Introduction, Baldip Sahota and Jacques Portal, 02:58 to 03:26; IWAS Wheelchair Fencing Coaching Video 2: Four Simple Actions, 01:07 and 06:49; Maestro Gary M. van der Wege, PARA-COACHING, 08:05 and 14:22 to 15:12
status: library

In Sahota's videos the back hand holds on to the wheelchair frame during the lunge so that the fencer does not fall over, and it is one of the checkpoints the coach looks at. For a [[Category B]] fencer the hold does more: they push with that arm to lunge and pull with it to recover, where a [[Category A]] fencer moves from the core. Van der Wege mentions that fencers grab the wheel ring or a custom sidebar handle, that there are many designs, and that each fencer eventually finds one that suits them. The hold is for balance and movement only; using this arm to attack or defend is not allowed (see [[Non-Sword Arm]]).

## Coaching Category A and B Fencers
short: The same techniques are taught to both categories, but the source of movement and the length of some exercises change.
source: IWAS Wheelchair Fencing Coaching Video 1: Introduction, Baldip Sahota and Jacques Portal, 02:10 to 04:03; Maestro Gary M. van der Wege, PARA-COACHING, 07:26 to 08:20
status: library

Sahota describes a [[Category A]] fencer as one with good core stability who leans forward and back and lunges using the trunk, without the arms. A [[Category B]] fencer moves mainly with the upper body and shoulders and holds the chair or an armrest, pushing to lunge and pulling to recover. In his words the rest of the technique is the same. One difference he asks coaches to watch: some exercises that ask a fencer to stay out in an extended position for a long time suit a Category A fencer, but a Category B fencer may not be able to hold them without falling. Van der Wege also names torso mobility as perhaps the main factor separating the two groups. See [[Individualisation]].

> Can I do the same long lunge holds as the others?
> We will keep the action and shorten the hold. Your back arm brings you home.

## Matching Actions to Mobility
short: Choosing the riposte, parry and attack each fencer is taught according to how far and how fast they can move in the chair.
source: Maestro Gary M. van der Wege, PARA-COACHING, 30:37 to 31:57 and 40:21 to 40:54
status: library

Van der Wege's weapon notes give examples. With high mobility foil fencers he says it is important to teach an indirect or compound [[Riposte]], because a lunging opponent exposes the back and flank. Lower mobility fencers may do better to concentrate on a simpler disengage riposte to the chest. For fencers with very limited hand function he suggests keeping pressure on the blade before releasing into the riposte. In sabre, a [[Category B]] fencer should develop as much speed as possible with the simple and the [[Beat]] attack, and use whatever movement the back arm can give. His general rule is simple actions at close distance and more complex ones when there is more distance. See [[Working Posture]] and [[Individualisation]].

## Attack Signal
short: A small move by the coach that tells the fencer when to attack in a lesson.
source: IWAS Wheelchair Fencing Coaching Video 2: Four Simple Actions, Baldip Sahota and Jacques Portal, 00:36 to 01:07, 03:05 to 03:18 and 06:34
status: library

Sahota's summary of coaching points starts here: the coach first gives a signal for the student to attack. In his own lessons he simply moves back slightly. He also invites with the hand, or with a slight movement up the blade, and often uses both. For the disengage he starts the parry slowly and then finishes fast, so the fencer has the chance to go round it and hit. The signal sets the right moment and tempo, and the coach can then check what follows. It is a form of [[Cue]] and works like an [[Invitation]] in a bout. See [[Presenting the Blade]] and [[Point Check]].

## Presenting the Blade
short: How the coach offers the blade or the target so the fencer can practise a parry or a hit at the right level.
source: IWAS Wheelchair Fencing Coaching Video 2: Four Simple Actions, Baldip Sahota and Jacques Portal, 01:36 to 02:01, 02:32 to 02:47 and 07:21
status: library

When Sahota attacks so that a beginner can take the [[Parry]], he presents his blade at an angle of about 40 degrees. As the fencer gets stronger he attacks straighter, the way an opponent would. After checking the student's hit, he presents the blade again, at a slight angle and aimed at the student's target, for the next parry. For the [[Counter-Riposte]] he offers his own riposte at a high angle, so the fencer can take the counter parry and hit again. In this way the coach controls how hard each step is. See [[Attack Signal]] and [[Individual Lesson]].

> Your attack is coming in at an angle. Is that what I will meet in a bout?
> Not yet. When your parry is strong, I will come straight.

## Point Check
short: The coach briefly blocks or meets the fencer's hit to check that the point arrives and sticks.
source: IWAS Wheelchair Fencing Coaching Video 2: Four Simple Actions, Baldip Sahota and Jacques Portal, 01:07 to 01:21, 02:15 and 06:49 to 07:06
status: library

In Sahota's lessons, after the fencer lunges the coach checks the point on the target to make sure it sticks to the body. He gives two reasons: it confirms the point is fixing, and it gives the right sequence and tempo for the next action, which may be a [[Parry]]. At each check he also looks at the guard above the shoulder, the straight arm and the angle of the body. The check is part of the coach's [[Error Correction]]: once a fault is seen, the coach stops the action, fixes it and runs the whole sequence again. See [[Seated Lunge]] and [[Attack Signal]].

## Tight Parry
short: A small parry that keeps the point inside the fencer's own body frame, from shoulder to shoulder and hip to hip.
source: IWAS Wheelchair Fencing Coaching Video 2: Four Simple Actions, Baldip Sahota and Jacques Portal, 04:20 to 04:54 and 07:06; IWAS Wheelchair Fencing Coaching Video 1: Introduction, 01:45 to 01:58; Maestro Gary M. van der Wege, PARA-COACHING, 27:38 to 28:04
status: library

Sahota's coaching point is that a [[Parry]] should stay within shoulder to shoulder and hip to hip, so the point stays inside the body frame. When the student's parry in his video is very wide and the blade is far off target, he asks for a much tighter one. His first video starts with simple quarte and sixte parries and the circular sixte. Van der Wege adds a foil detail: because the fencers are so close, many parried hits land off target, and a strong downward parry can carry the opponent's point into the legs. See [[Riposte]], [[Four Simple Actions]] and [[Target Area]].

## Four Simple Actions
short: A short WPF lesson sequence of four basic foil actions, used to build sound habits from the start.
source: IWAS Wheelchair Fencing Coaching Video 2: Four Simple Actions, Baldip Sahota and Jacques Portal, 00:04 to 00:36 and 06:34 to 07:21; IWAS Wheelchair Fencing Coaching Video 1: Introduction, 11:55 to 13:23
status: library

The second WPF coaching video names them: the direct [[Attack]]; the quarte parry and [[Riposte]]; the quarte parry, a first counter parry in quarte and a riposte; and an attack against the coach's parry, finished with a disengage. The aim is for the student to do each action at the right speed, to the right area, with the hand, guard and body positions correct. The first video ends with a three-phase version: the fencer attacks, the coach parries and ripostes, and the fencer takes a parry and makes the first [[Counter-Riposte]], once with a lunge and once with a lean back when the coach ripostes deeper. See [[Point Check]] and [[Tight Parry]].

## Sabre Warm-Up Drills
short: Short paired drills at the start of a seated sabre lesson: cuts, taps on the blade, changing distance and recovery.
source: Sabre Wheelchair Fencing Coaching: Demonstrating Advanced Warm-up Drills, World Abilitysport coaching series, 00:00 to 01:17, 02:42 to 03:28, 04:20 and 05:34 to 06:07
status: library

The video builds up in steps. First come direct cuts with the coach changing distance, so the fencer learns to finish each cut. Next the coach holds the blade horizontal, and the fencer taps it twice and cuts to the head, in a tempo of one, two, three. Further drills use the fingers to cut to the head and then the side of the mask, and later add a [[Remise]] and a [[Feint]]. When the fencer recovers by taking a parry instead of returning with the point in line, the coach deceives it and hits, then asks for the recovery with the point in line. The coach also changes the line of the final action so the fencer must pick the right parry. The actions are kept steady; speed comes later. See [[Sabre]] and [[Changing Distance in the Lesson]].

## Chair Warm-Up
short: Upper body exercises done seated to raise blood flow before fencing.
source: Maestro Gary M. van der Wege, PARA-COACHING, 23:23 to 24:17
status: library

Van der Wege notes that it can be harder to get the blood flowing through the body when warming up a seated fencer. He suggests a set of arm and trunk movements done in the chair: tarzans, bear hugs, neck rolls, butterflies, and backstrokes with torso twists, followed by the lunge and the retreat. Massage sticks can also help bring blood to the muscles and keep them supple. He then advises getting the fencer warmed up and moving in the chair as much as possible before the lesson starts. See [[Seated Footwork]], [[Muscle Relaxation]] and [[Sabre Warm-Up Drills]].

## Practice Chair
short: A simple chair and frame set-up a club can use to start lessons before it has competition equipment.
source: Maestro Gary M. van der Wege, PARA-COACHING, 13:11 to 13:25, 15:47 to 16:01, 16:48 to 18:15 and 19:13 to 19:29
status: library

Van der Wege says a new fencer does not need all the competition equipment to begin. A common folding wheelchair can be used, perhaps with the back cut down and one side handle removed. With no wheelchair at all, a straight back chair can be used, even sideways at a slight angle to the coach, to give a little working distance. He shows a homemade frame of plywood with eye bolts and ratchet straps. His safety notes: never put the ratchet strap over the fencer's feet, since someone without feeling there could break a bone, and take care not to clamp air filled tyres too hard. Metal parts in contact with the jacket should be insulated. See [[Frame]], [[Wheelchair Fixing]] and [[Fencing Wheelchair]].

## Getting Started in Para Fencing
short: What a coach prepares before the first lessons: knowing each athlete, asking before helping, and an accessible club.
source: Maestro Gary M. van der Wege, PARA-COACHING, 05:30 to 05:54, 09:29 to 10:53, 11:09 to 11:50, 12:05 to 12:18 and 42:39 to 43:31
status: library

Van der Wege advises coaches first to understand the impairment of each athlete: to see where they may fit in [[Classification]], to be ready for any medical emergency, and to know their limits in training. As a general rule, do not touch or assist a person without their permission; ask first. A fencer with no use of the abdominal muscles can fall forward if they lose grip and balance, and a little help back upright is usually welcome. Spinal damage can affect sweating, so watch for overheating. He also reminds clubs that being wheelchair accessible means more than a ramp and grab bars, and suggests asking an architect. Above all, the coach's job is to let the person feel like an athlete. See [[Losing Balance]] and [[Talent Identification]].

> Do you want a hand with the strap?
> Thanks for asking. I can do it, just hold the chair still.

## Mixed Seated Training
short: Able-bodied fencers training seated alongside para fencers, which helps both groups and the club.
source: Maestro Gary M. van der Wege, PARA-COACHING, 00:50 to 01:16 and 43:31 to 44:38
status: library

Van der Wege recalls that the classic coaching book by Szabo used seated fencing to train engagements and changes of line safely, and to teach relaxed shoulders and trunk. He describes seated fencing as blade work coupled with distance and tactics. He urges coaches to bring para athletes and able-bodied fencers together as much as possible: able-bodied fencers usually enjoy fencing seated and soon respect its speed and difficulty. On recruiting, he warns that clubs compete with many other adaptive sports, and that some people in rehabilitation do not want to be linked with wheelchairs, so a club may prefer to call its program chair, seated or para fencing. See [[Paired Exercise]], [[Group Lesson]] and [[Muscle Relaxation]].
