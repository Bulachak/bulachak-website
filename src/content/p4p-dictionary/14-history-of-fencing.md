## Rapier
short: The long cut-and-thrust sword of the sixteenth century, the weapon from which the sport's thrusting play grew.
source: E. Castle, Schools and Masters of Fence (1885), Introduction, pp. 5 to 12; A. Hutton, Old Sword-Play (1892), Introduction, pp. 1 to 4
status: library

Castle divides the history of the art into three periods, and the first, from the early sixteenth century into the first years of the seventeenth, is the age of the rapier. Armour had gone out of private life, and men relied on agility and cunning instead of heavy blows. Every master taught his own favourite tricks, and fencers dreamed of a secret thrust and a "universal parry" that would beat anything. The rapier was long and double-edged, often used with a dagger, buckler or cloak in the left hand. Hutton notes that the edges were used at first but gave way to the swifter point. As blades shortened and lightened, the rapier gave way to the [[Small Sword]], and its thrusting principles passed to the [[Foil]] and the [[Épée]].

## Small Sword
short: The light, thrust-only court sword of the late seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, the direct ancestor of the foil.
source: E. Castle, Schools and Masters of Fence (1885), Introduction, pp. 5 to 6, and chapter XV, pp. 238 to 239
status: library

When gentlemen stopped being soldiers by default, the sword became part of their dress. Castle dates the absolute split between the court sword and the military sword from this period, both descended from the [[Rapier]]. The French were the first to drop cutting entirely, and they adopted the triangular fluted blade, which Castle places in general use from about 1650 to 1660. A three-cornered blade on a very simple hilt made the small sword, a weapon "managed by the wrist" and used only for thrusting. Through the whole eighteenth century it was cultivated almost alone, and Castle calls that age the birth of modern swordsmanship. Its practice weapon was the [[Fleuret]], and its rules of the hit live on in [[Foil]] and [[Épée]].

## Fleuret
short: The buttoned practice blade of the French schools, and the origin of the word and weapon called foil.
source: E. Castle, Schools and Masters of Fence (1885), chapter IX, pp. 138 to 139, and chapter X, p. 144; Le Sieur de Liancour, Le Maistre d'armes (1686), as quoted by Castle
status: library

Rebated practice swords are centuries old, but Castle explains that once the French gave up cutting, flat practice blades had no purpose, and the fleuret was made for the thrust alone. He gives two word origins: "foil" from the old French fouler, to turn back, and fleuret from the button's likeness to a flower bud. The early French foil kept a clumsy rapier-style guard until about the middle of the eighteenth century, although it was already held with the fingers on the grip. Liancour (1686) used different foils for master and pupil, the pupil's having no guard so that he learned to parry with the strong part of the blade. The modern [[Foil]] keeps this practice weapon's role and its limited [[Target Area]]. See also [[Diagrammatic Fencing]].

## Diagrammatic Fencing
short: Castle's name for foil play once it became an end in itself rather than a rehearsal for real sword fighting.
source: E. Castle, Schools and Masters of Fence (1885), Introduction, pp. 6 to 7; Sieur de Labat, The Art of Fencing, or the Use of the Small Sword (1696, trans. A. Mahon 1734), chap. XXXI
status: library

Labat already saw that school rules and real fights differed: with the foil, thrusts counted only on the body, while with the sword they counted everywhere. Castle went further. For centuries blunts stood for sharps, but in his own day "foil play alone" had become the goal. Light foils allowed complicated attacks and parries that would fail with a real sword, so the play became artificial but, under a strict code for hits, more perfect. He called foil practice "diagrammatic" fencing, freed of outside disturbances. That code is where [[Origins of Priority|priority]] begins, and the reduced [[Target Area]] of the [[Foil]] is its clearest trace today.

> Why can I only hit the trunk in foil?
> Because the foil grew up as a schooling weapon, and the old masters counted only the hits that mattered most.

## Origins of Priority
short: How the salle's conventions for judging double hits grew into the modern right of way.
source: E. Castle, Schools and Masters of Fence (1885), Introduction, p. 6, and chapter XI, p. 159; L. Rondelle, Foil and Sabre: A Grammar of Fencing (1892), Rules Governing Amateur Competitions and Double Hits, pp. 176 to 180; R. Cohen, By the Sword (2002), Prologue, p. xx
status: library

Castle wrote that complicated foil play made a code necessary for "settling the value of hits" when both fencers were touched, since in real fighting a double hit was good for neither. In eighteenth century schools custom already asked a fencer to avoid time hits and to riposte only as the opponent recovered. Rondelle's rules of 1892 show the code working: a double hit from equal simultaneous attacks was annulled, a badly judged time thrust gave the point to the attacker, and a stop thrust on a faulty attack counted for the one who made it. Cohen describes the result as a right of way: a fencer who starts an attack must miss or be parried before the opponent may reply. Today this is [[Priority]], applied to the [[Double Touch]] and [[Simultaneous Action]].

## Military Sabre
short: The curved cutting sword of the light cavalry, taught in army schools before it became a sporting weapon.
source: E. Castle, Schools and Masters of Fence (1885), chapter XIV, p. 220; R. Cohen, By the Sword (2002), p. 95; War Department, Provisional Regulations for Saber Exercise (1907), paras. 6 to 7
status: library

Cohen traces the sabre to the eighteenth century, as a curved weapon descended from the scimitar, handier than the heavy cavalry broadsword. It became the national weapon of Hungary and other armies took it up. Castle describes how many new light cavalry corps brought in the Austrian system, where the slicing of the light curved Hungarian sabre replaced the old chopping. The United States regulations of 1907 base the mounted soldier's swordsmanship on thorough instruction on foot, with masks, plastrons and padded gauntlets for practice. The sporting [[Sabre]] keeps the cut. Its target above the hips is often explained by cavalry never striking at legs, but Cohen says leg cuts stayed valid in combat and in competition into the 1910s. See [[Santelli and the Hungarian Sabre]].

## Santelli and the Hungarian Sabre
short: How an Italian master's lighter sabre and footwork, brought to Budapest in 1896, shaped modern sabre fencing.
source: R. Cohen, By the Sword (2002), pp. 391 to 394
status: library

Cohen writes that the Italian Radaelli invented the light sporting sabre. Hungary skipped the first modern Olympics in 1896 and held its own Millennium tournament, where the sabre was won by a young Italian army officer, Italo Santelli, who used a lighter sabre and quick footwork. Invited to stay, he moved to Budapest that September. Hungarian sabreurs of the time had little footwork, and he changed how they cut. He taught that avoiding an attack by moving out of [[Fencing Distance|distance]] was not shameful, and the lighter blade allowed feints and faster hands. This blend of Italian and Hungarian play grew out of the [[Military Sabre]] and led to the sport [[Sabre]] of the twentieth century.

## Duelling Épée
short: The late nineteenth century return to fencing with the real duelling sword, capped, which became the épée.
source: R. Cohen, By the Sword (2002), pp. 198 to 200 and 442 to 443; E. Castle, Schools and Masters of Fence (1885), Introduction, p. 8, note
status: library

Cohen writes that by mid century duellists were mostly prepared with ordinary foil lessons. In 1862 the Baron de Bazancourt argued that the salle should imitate real fighting, and Paris masters began to teach with standard duelling swords whose tips were capped. Between 1884 and 1893 La Marche, Jacob, Baudry and Spinnewyn wrote that new technique was needed: the guard deflects attacks, parry and riposte become one movement, and the hand and wrist are targets, so the guard grew larger. Clubs for épée alone appeared. To show hits, the point d'arrêt came in 1883 and a triple point in 1906. The modern [[Épée]] keeps this duelling logic and has no [[Priority]]. Compare [[Diagrammatic Fencing]].

> Why is there no right of way in épée?
> Because it was built to copy the duel, where the only rule was to hit without being hit.

## Italian School
short: The sixteenth and seventeenth century Italian masters whose treatises laid the foundations of modern fencing.
source: E. Castle, Schools and Masters of Fence (1885), Introduction, p. 7, and chapters II, VI and VII; A. Hutton, Old Sword-Play (1892), Introduction, p. 2
status: library

Hutton calls the Italians the original teachers of the art, and names the Italian and the French the "two great and only schools" of fence. Marozzo (1536) still taught many fanciful guards. Agrippa (1553) cut them to four and gave them numbers (see [[Numbered Guards]]) and made freer use of the point. Castle names Fabris (1606), Giganti (1606) and Capo Ferro (1610) as the masters on whose foundations the science was built. Giganti and Capo Ferro explained the [[Development of the Lunge|lunge]], and the Italians long kept the single time, parrying and hitting in one action. The [[French School]] grew out of this teaching and separated from it.

## Spanish Destreza
short: The geometric Spanish science of fence founded by Carranza and Narvaez, built around an imaginary circle.
source: E. Castle, Schools and Masters of Fence (1885), chapter IV, pp. 67 to 70
status: library

Castle reports that Carranza finished his book in 1569, but it only circulated from 1582. He was called the inventor of the science of arms, and his pupil Don Luis Pacheco de Narvaez spread the method through a long series of books from 1600. The fencers fell on guard out of reach, at the ends of the diameter of an imaginary circle drawn on the ground, arm and sword straight. Crossing that circle brought a fencer within measure, so careful angled steps decided who could hit and who could be stopped. Castle found nothing like a lunge in it, and he was critical of its geometry. Its lasting idea is control of [[Fencing Distance|distance]]. Compare [[Measure in the Old Schools]] and the [[Italian School]].

## French School
short: The school that grew in France from Italian teaching and gave the sport its separate parry and riposte, the foil, and most of its words.
source: E. Castle, Schools and Masters of Fence (1885), chapters VIII to XI; R. Cohen, By the Sword (2002), p. 72; A. Hutton, Old Sword-Play (1892), Introduction, p. 2
status: library

Castle traces the Paris Académie d'Armes to the reign of Charles IX, with its first privileges from Henri III; Cohen gives 1567 as the year Charles IX recognised it. For a long time it taught the Bolognese Italian method. In the seventeenth century the French shortened and lightened their swords, dropped the cut, and began to parry and hit in two separate movements, as Besnard (1653) shows. Le Perche stressed the riposte, Liancour (1686) fixed a lunge much like the modern one, and Labat (1696) wrote the classic small sword text. Hutton notes that the light French sword gained in "precision and grace". From this school come the [[Small Sword]], the [[Fleuret]] and the [[Origins of the Riposte|riposte]]. Compare the [[Italian School]].

## Development of the Lunge
short: How the old passing steps were replaced by the lunge, first glimpsed by Viggiani and explained by Giganti and Capo Ferro.
source: E. Castle, Schools and Masters of Fence (1885), Introduction, p. 8; chapter III, p. 66; chapter IV, p. 70; chapter VII, pp. 106 to 108; chapter X, p. 144
status: library

Early swordsmen attacked by passing, stepping through with the back foot. Castle sees the first clear sign of a lunge in Viggiani's punta sopramano, a thrust made with "one great step" of the right foot, in a book finished in 1560 and printed in 1575. Giganti (1606) first explained its advantage and used it for most attacks, and Capo Ferro (1610) described its mechanism more carefully. Castle adds that the method of lunging and recovering was the last point the masters fixed, and the Spanish system had nothing like it. In France, Liancour (1686) kept the back foot flat and the front knee over the instep, much as today. For wheelchair fencers, the modern [[Lunge]] becomes the [[Seated Lunge]], which delivers the [[Attack]].

## Measure in the Old Schools
short: How the old masters taught distance, from "time, distance, and proportion" to Capo Ferro's wide and close measure.
source: E. Castle, Schools and Masters of Fence (1885), Introduction, p. 8, and chapter VII, p. 107; Sieur de Labat, The Art of Fencing, or the Use of the Small Sword (1696, trans. A. Mahon 1734), chap. II
status: library

Castle writes that the "time, distance, and proportion" of the sixteenth century Anglo-Italian masters became time, measure and guard, and that keeping the proper measure is the first principle of all fencing: stay out of easy reach when defending, and never attack from too far. Capo Ferro (1610) measured from the point of the sword to the opponent's body. In wide measure you could hit only by lunging, in close measure by leaning the body forward. Labat set the guard's feet about two feet apart, because a longer stance would put you within reach when your opponent was not, and called measure one of the principal parts of fencing. Today it is [[Fencing Distance]]. Compare [[Spanish Destreza]] and [[Tempo in the Old Schools]].

> In the chair the distance is fixed. Does measure still matter?
> More than ever. You cannot step back, so you must know exactly where your reach ends.

## Tempo in the Old Schools
short: The old idea of time in fencing, from Silver's "true times" to Capo Ferro's time as the length of one action.
source: G. Silver, Paradoxes of Defence (1599), p. 27; E. Castle, Schools and Masters of Fence (1885), Introduction, p. 9; chapter VI, p. 96; chapter VII, p. 107
status: library

Castle notes that, unlike measure, time was one of the first things masters understood: use as few and as small movements as possible, and match them to the opponent's to seize the least opening. George Silver (1599) listed four true times, beginning with "the time of the hand", then hand and body, then adding the feet. He called the times that start with the foot false, since the hand is quicker. Capo Ferro defined a time as the period of any single action of sword or foot, and Docciolini wrote of tempo, contra tempo and mezzo tempo. The modern unit is [[Fencing Time]], and seizing the moment is [[Choice of Time]]. Compare [[Measure in the Old Schools]].

## Origins of the Riposte
short: How parry and return, once one action in the single time, became two separate actions in the French school.
source: E. Castle, Schools and Masters of Fence (1885), Introduction, p. 9; chapter VI, pp. 99 to 100; chapter IX, pp. 138 and 141
status: library

Castle writes that only about two centuries before him were parries treated as truly different from attacks. Fabris (1606) argued for the single time, stesso tempo, in which a good parry also hits, against the double time of parrying first and returning after. With the long rapier this made sense. As swords grew lighter, the French found it better to parry first and then return, and Besnard (1653) shows them fencing "en deux temps". The same separation produced the reprise, renewing an attack the opponent parried without answering. Le Perche, for Castle the father of the modern French school, insisted on the riposte. Today these are the [[Parry]], the [[Riposte]] and the [[Renewed Attack]].

> Why do we parry and then riposte instead of doing both at once?
> Light blades made it possible, and it is safer to close the line before you hit.

## Numbered Guards
short: Why the parries are counted prime, seconde, tierce and quarte, a numbering that began with Agrippa's rapier guards.
source: E. Castle, Schools and Masters of Fence (1885), chapter II, pp. 46 to 47, and chapter XI, pp. 161 to 162
status: library

Camillo Agrippa (1553) threw out the fanciful names of earlier guards and kept four, numbered prima, seconda, terza and quarta. His first guard was simply where the hand ended after drawing a long [[Rapier]]: above the head. Castle uses this to explain a puzzle for beginners: the actions taught first are called fourth and third (carte and tierce), and the rarest is called first. The names were made for rapier play, partly adjusted later for the small sword and foil. In 1766 Danet tried to renumber the actions in their natural order for small sword play, but his effort stayed alone and the old names survived. The guards became the modern [[On Guard]] position and the named positions of the [[Parry]].

## Fencing Masks in History
short: The wire mask of the eighteenth century, resisted at first, which made faster and freer fencing possible.
source: E. Castle, Schools and Masters of Fence (1885), chapter XI, pp. 159 and 169, and chapter XV, p. 246; H. Pollock, F. C. Grove and C. Prevost, Fencing (1890), Introduction, pp. 23 to 25; R. Cohen, By the Sword (2002), p. 75
status: library

Castle credits La Boëssière with inventing the wire mask, which he tried to bring into fashion about 1750, tied on with strings. Cohen also gives about 1750 and says three masters had lost an eye shortly before. Yet masks were not adopted everywhere until the end of the eighteenth century. Fashionable schools called them unnecessary for good players, and Pollock and his co-authors write that wearing one was thought bad form, suggesting distrust of the opponent. Without a mask the fencer kept the head back and the weight on the rear leg, so play was slow and cramped. Once masks spread, fencing became freer and quicker. Today's [[Mask]] is the end of that story, worn in every [[Salle d'Armes]].

## Salle d'Armes
short: The fencing room or school of arms, from the Tudor halls to the eighteenth century salles and Angelo's school in London.
source: E. Castle, Schools and Masters of Fence (1885), chapter I, pp. 18 to 19; chapter XI, pp. 158 to 159; chapter XIV, p. 212
status: library

Early English masters held their public trials in theatres, halls and yards, and their schools had a rough name. Castle describes how, from the early reign of Louis XIV, the Paris schools became as much schools of deportment as of fighting, with customs that governed the assault and valued style over vigour. Wounding a partner disgraced a fencer for life. In London, Domenico Angelo, born at Leghorn in 1716 and trained in Paris, founded a school whose name became a household word, kept by three generations of his family for a century. His book, L'École des Armes, appeared in 1763. The club where you fence is still a salle, run by a [[Master of Arms]], beginning each bout with the [[Fencer's Salute]].

## Master of Arms
short: The title of a fully qualified fencing teacher, once won by public trial in the old corporations of masters.
source: E. Castle, Schools and Masters of Fence (1885), chapter I, pp. 17 to 18 and 31; R. Cohen, By the Sword (2002), pp. 72 and 221
status: library

Castle quotes a 1615 account of London schools: Henry VIII had made the teachers a corporation, and a pupil began as a scholar, became a provost of defence, and finally a master, each step won by public trial at several weapons called prizes. No one else could teach. In a Spanish document from Perpignan the steps were student, licentiate, and "Master of Arms". Cohen adds that the French academy required at least six years' study under a recognised master before membership, and that George Patton was the first to hold the United States Army title Master of the Sword. The master's lasting work is the [[Individual Lesson]] in the [[Salle d'Armes]], in the tradition of the [[French School]].

> What makes someone a master and not just a coach?
> Traditionally, years as a pupil and a provost, and then passing a public test before other masters.

## Origins of the Salute
short: The courteous opening of an assault, first named "reverence" in the seventeenth century and fixed by the Paris Académie d'Armes in 1888.
source: E. Castle, Schools and Masters of Fence (1885), chapter IX, pp. 138 to 139, and chapter XI, p. 159; H. Pollock, F. C. Grove and C. Prevost, Fencing (1890), chapter VI, The Salute
status: library

Castle writes that Besnard (1653) seems to have been the first to teach the courteous practice of the salute, which he called "reverence". The habit belongs to the change he dates to the early reign of Louis XIV, when schools of arms began to value manners as much as fighting. Until then each school practised it with its own differences of detail. Pollock and his co-authors record that the Académie d'Armes of Paris, founded in 1886, put the salute into official form at the end of 1888, as a way for both fencers to show courtesy to each other and to the spectators. The short modern [[Fencer's Salute]] keeps that meaning, made in every [[Salle d'Armes]] before the [[Bout]].
