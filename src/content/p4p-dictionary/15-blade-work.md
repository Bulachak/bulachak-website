## Line
short: One part of the target, named by where it lies against your own blade: inside or outside, high or low.
source: D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), chapter Terminology, PDF pp. 41 and 43; L. Rondelle, Foil and Sabre: A Grammar of Fencing (1892), pp. 19 to 20; S. Jefferson, Fencing: A Beginner's Guide, Volume 1, Herdwick Fencing Club (undated), glossary, pp. 35 and 36; Sabre Wheelchair Fencing Coaching: Demonstrating Advanced Warm-up Drills, World Abilitysport coaching series, 04:20
status: library

Tyshler defines the fencing lines as a convention for the sides of space inside and outside, above and below, the fencer's own blade. His sector is the part of the target bounded by a vertical and a horizontal line that cross at the centre of the guard. Rondelle counts four lines: quarte (inside), sixte (outside, also called tierce), high and low, and says engagements are taken only in quarte and sixte. Jefferson splits high from low halfway down the trunk and ties each line to a guard (see [[Parry Positions]]). In a seated sabre drill the coach changes the line of their final action so the fencer must pick the right [[Parry]]. In wheelchair fencing no hit counts below the line of the hips, and at foil the target is the trunk only (see [[Target Area]]).

## Engagement
short: Contact between the two blades, often the starting point of an attack.
source: D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), chapter Terminology, PDF pp. 39, 42 and 43; The Basics of Fencing, adapted from A Parent's Guide to Fencing (2007), glossary, p. 1; G. Heintz, Theory of Fencing with the Foil in Form of a Catechism (1890), p. 5; L. Rondelle, Foil and Sabre: A Grammar of Fencing (1892), pp. 15, 25 and 202
status: library

Tyshler's term, soedinenie, means simply the contact of the blades. To hold the engagement means to be covered against a direct hit, and a change of engagement moves the weapon from one line to another. The Basics of Fencing adds that engagement is often the prelude to an attack. The older books treat it as an action: Heintz and Rondelle take it by passing the point under the opponent's blade and covering oneself on the other side, with the middle third of the blade touching. Rondelle notes that at sabre the blades meet edge to edge, and that tierce is the only useful engagement between two right-handers. Attacks from the engagement include the [[Disengage]], the [[Coupé]] and the [[Glide]]. See also [[Prise de Fer]].

## Lunge
short: The attacking movement that carries the extended arm forward to the target. In the wheelchair it becomes a lean of the trunk.
source: D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), chapter Terminology, PDF p. 39; L. Rondelle, Foil and Sabre: A Grammar of Fencing (1892), p. 18; R. Howard, Essentials of Fencing Technique, in Key Skills in Blade Fencing (undated), PDF pp. 10 to 11; IWAS Wheelchair Fencing Coaching Video 2: Four Simple Actions, Baldip Sahota and Jacques Portal, 01:07 to 01:21 and 06:34 to 06:49
status: library

Tyshler defines the vypad as a movement forward from the on guard by a push of the rear leg, landing on the front leg. Rondelle starts it from the extended arm. Howard teaches beginners three steps in order: the thrust first, then the kick of the front foot with the push of the back leg, then the back arm thrown back as a counterweight. He credits Capo Ferro with the first picture of the lunge. In the fixed chair there is no footwork. The WPF video looks for the arm straight at the end, the guard above the shoulder, the body leaning at an angle towards the hit, and the back hand holding the frame. See [[Seated Lunge]], [[Trunk Lean]], [[Back Hand Hold]] and [[Lifting from the Seat]].

> Where does my back hand go when I lunge?
> On the frame, so you do not fall over.

## Disengage
short: An attack that goes round the opponent's blade into the opposite line, usually passing under it.
source: L. Rondelle, Foil and Sabre: A Grammar of Fencing (1892), pp. 26 to 27; D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), chapter Terminology, PDF p. 42; R. Howard, Essentials of Fencing Technique, in Key Skills in Blade Fencing (undated), PDF pp. 15 to 16; The Basics of Fencing, adapted from A Parent's Guide to Fencing (2007), glossary, p. 1; IWAS Wheelchair Fencing Coaching Video 2: Four Simple Actions, Baldip Sahota and Jacques Portal, 00:18, 03:05 to 03:18 and 04:54
status: library

Rondelle calls the disengagement an attack in the line opposite the engagement, the point passing under the opponent's forearm by the fingers and wrist only. Tyshler's perevod goes round the opponent's blade, guard or armed arm. Howard notes that the name first meant releasing one blade from another, and separates the derobement, which deceives an attempt to take the blade, from the trompement, which deceives a parry. The Basics of Fencing uses disengage for that evasion alone. In the last of the WPF [[Four Simple Actions]], the coach starts their parry slowly and finishes fast, so the seated fencer has time to go round it. See [[Engagement]], [[Coupé]] and [[Simple Attack]].

> My disengage landed, but way up on the quarte side.
> Go round the parry and hit square.

## Counter-Disengage
short: A disengage that follows the opponent's disengage round, so the blades end up back in their first lines.
source: L. Rondelle, Foil and Sabre: A Grammar of Fencing (1892), p. 187; S. Jefferson, Fencing: A Beginner's Guide, Volume 1, Herdwick Fencing Club (undated), glossary, p. 33
status: library

Rondelle describes it as deceiving the opponent's disengagement with a similar movement. Both fencers are engaged in quarte; one disengages into sixte, and the other follows round from sixte into quarte with good [[Opposition]] and better command of the blade. He treats it as a time thrust, made on a disengage or a feint of disengage. He calls it a grave error to think it can be made on a change of engagement: that, he says, is a disengagement that deceives the change. Jefferson's modern glossary defines the counter-disengagement as exactly that case, an indirect action that deceives a change of engagement. The usage has moved. See [[Disengage]] and [[Engagement]].

## Coupé
short: A disengage made over the opponent's point instead of under the blade. Also called the cut-over.
source: L. Rondelle, Foil and Sabre: A Grammar of Fencing (1892), p. 27; G. Heintz, Theory of Fencing with the Foil in Form of a Catechism (1890), p. 11; R. Howard, Essentials of Fencing Technique, in Key Skills in Blade Fencing (undated), PDF p. 16; D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), chapter Terminology, PDF p. 42, and foil chapter, PDF pp. 52 to 53
status: library

Rondelle defines the cut-over as a disengagement over the opponent's point, made with the fingers only. It is drawn out when the opponent presses the blade too hard or holds the point low and out of line. Heintz adds that it also works when the fencers are too close, and as a riposte. Howard credits it to Andre de Liancour in the mid 1600s. Tyshler's term is perenos, an attack that goes round the point of the opponent's blade. His foil chapter uses it against an opponent in a low position, since the blade then avoids meeting an upper parry, and describes attacks with a series of coupés. See [[Disengage]], [[Simple Attack]] and [[Direct and Indirect Riposte]].

> Why the coupé and not a disengage?
> His point was low and he kept pressing your blade.

## Glide
short: An attack that slides along the opponent's blade, keeping contact, and hits in the same line.
source: L. Rondelle, Foil and Sabre: A Grammar of Fencing (1892), pp. 43 and 204; R. Howard, Essentials of Fencing Technique, in Key Skills in Blade Fencing (undated), PDF pp. 6 and 15; G. Heintz, Theory of Fencing with the Foil in Form of a Catechism (1890), p. 9
status: library

Rondelle calls the glide the action of sliding along the opponent's blade while keeping it in [[Opposition]]. He says it is really a feint of a direct thrust, to be made slyly, without alarming the opponent. Howard, who uses the French coulé, counts it among the attacks on the blade: the blade runs quickly and lightly along the opponent's and lands in the same line, best from sixte or quarte. Heintz's graze runs along the opponent's blade from foible to forte, arm extended and in opposition. At sabre, Rondelle starts one arm cut with a glide along the opponent's weapon. Howard's example of a direct riposte is the riposte by glide (see [[Direct and Indirect Riposte]]). Compare the [[Beat]], which strikes the blade instead.

## Bind
short: Taking the opponent's blade and carrying it diagonally from a high line to the opposite low line, or the reverse.
source: L. Rondelle, Foil and Sabre: A Grammar of Fencing (1892), pp. 62 and 185; R. Howard, Essentials of Fencing Technique, in Key Skills in Blade Fencing (undated), PDF p. 13; S. Jefferson, Fencing: A Beginner's Guide, Volume 1, Herdwick Fencing Club (undated), glossary, pp. 32 to 33
status: library

Rondelle describes the bind as taking possession of the opponent's blade, forte on foible, and carrying it from high quarte into low sixte, or from high sixte into low quarte, then lunging under the hand. He adds that the original bind was a full circle through every line, given up by most masters, so the bind of his day is half of it. Jefferson's glossary gives the same diagonal path. Howard names the binds in sixte, quarte, septime and octave, notes that they can be taken from other positions too, and says they are now often called semicircular diagonal transfers. The bind is one of the takings of the blade (see [[Prise de Fer]]). Compare the [[Croisé]] and the [[Envelopment]].

## Croisé
short: Taking the opponent's blade from a high line to a low line, or the reverse, on the same side.
source: S. Jefferson, Fencing: A Beginner's Guide, Volume 1, Herdwick Fencing Club (undated), glossary, p. 34; R. Howard, Essentials of Fencing Technique, in Key Skills in Blade Fencing (undated), PDF p. 14; L. Rondelle, Foil and Sabre: A Grammar of Fencing (1892), p. 185
status: library

Jefferson's glossary defines the croisé as taking the opponent's foible from high to low line, or the reverse, on the same side as the engagement. That is what separates it from the [[Bind]], which crosses to the opposite side. Howard calls it a small bind, usually taken from sixte down to octave, where it has the most controlling power, and says it is now often called a vertical semicircular transfer. Rondelle's cross is a different action. It goes over the blade like the bind, but instead of holding the blade it drives it away hard to open a large gap, against fencers who extend the arm too far and on the riposte as the opponent recovers. See [[Prise de Fer]] and [[Octave]].

## Envelopment
short: Taking the opponent's blade round a full circle while keeping contact, back to the line where it started.
source: S. Jefferson, Fencing: A Beginner's Guide, Volume 1, Herdwick Fencing Club (undated), glossary, p. 35; R. Howard, Essentials of Fencing Technique, in Key Skills in Blade Fencing (undated), PDF p. 14; D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), chapter Terminology, PDF p. 40, and épée chapter, PDF p. 68
status: library

Jefferson's glossary defines it as taking the opponent's foible with a complete circle, keeping contact throughout. Howard asks for a very small, tight circle round the opponent's guard, starting and ending at the top of it, and notes that envelopments are now called circular transfers, so the envelopment in sixte becomes circle six. Tyshler's zavyazyvanie is a kind of taking that moves the opponent's blade through different planes. His épée chapter stresses unbroken contact of the strong or middle part of the blade on the opponent's weak part, and shows envelopments that finish with a hit to the leg. In wheelchair épée the leg is not target (see [[Target Area]]). See [[Prise de Fer]] and [[Bind]].

## Parry Positions
short: The eight standard placings of hand and blade, prime to octave, each covering one part of the target.
source: L. Rondelle, Foil and Sabre: A Grammar of Fencing (1892), pp. 20 to 24; S. Jefferson, Fencing: A Beginner's Guide, Volume 1, Herdwick Fencing Club (undated), pp. 22 to 23; D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), foil, épée and sabre chapters, PDF pp. 53, 69 and 82; G. Heintz, Theory of Fencing with the Foil in Form of a Catechism (1890), pp. 5 to 7; IWAS Wheelchair Fencing Coaching Video 2: Four Simple Actions, Baldip Sahota and Jacques Portal, 07:06
status: library

Rondelle lists eight parries: prime, seconde, tierce, quarte, quinte, sixte, septime and octave. He pairs them by area: prime and quarte for high quarte, sixte and tierce for high sixte, quinte and septime for low quarte, seconde and octave for low sixte. Jefferson's guide calls the same eight the fencing positions, also known as lines. Russian textbooks number them, so Tyshler writes the 4th and 6th parries for quarte and sixte. Heintz has no septime or octave: he calls those parries lower quarte and lower tierce. The set also changes by weapon (see [[Quinte]]). In the WPF video the coach wants every parry kept within shoulder to shoulder and hip to hip (see [[Tight Parry]]). See also [[Line]] and [[Parry]].

## Prime
short: The first position: a parry on the inside, with the hand turned over and the thumb down.
source: S. Jefferson, Fencing: A Beginner's Guide, Volume 1, Herdwick Fencing Club (undated), glossary, p. 37; L. Rondelle, Foil and Sabre: A Grammar of Fencing (1892), p. 21; G. Heintz, Theory of Fencing with the Foil in Form of a Catechism (1890), pp. 5 to 7; D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), foil, épée and sabre chapters, PDF pp. 53, 62 and 82 to 83
status: library

Jefferson's glossary calls prime a high line, pronated guard on the non-sword-arm side. Rondelle forms it against a thrust in high quarte: the thumb turns down, the forearm rises level close to the forehead, and the point threatens the opponent's low line. Heintz separates a low prime, with the blade vertical and the hand in front of the left shoulder, from a high prime with the hand beside the head. Tyshler calls it the 1st parry and treats it as a low one. At foil he says top fencers sometimes use it in place of the 7th, and at épée in close fighting. At sabre it is one of two low parries kept for chosen moments. See [[Parry Positions]], [[Septime]] and [[Seconde]].

## Seconde
short: The second position: a low parry on the outside, with the hand pronated.
source: S. Jefferson, Fencing: A Beginner's Guide, Volume 1, Herdwick Fencing Club (undated), glossary, p. 37; L. Rondelle, Foil and Sabre: A Grammar of Fencing (1892), pp. 22 and 206; G. Heintz, Theory of Fencing with the Foil in Form of a Catechism (1890), pp. 5 to 7; D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), foil, épée and sabre chapters, PDF pp. 53, 69, 82 and 84
status: library

Jefferson's glossary calls seconde a low line, pronated guard on the sword-arm side. Rondelle forms it against a thrust in low sixte: hand and point drop a little in a half circle, and blade, wrist and forearm finish in one level line, nails down, the point threatening the low line. In his sabre chapter, point thrusts in the low line are parried by seconde. Tyshler calls it the 2nd parry. At foil top fencers sometimes use it in place of the 8th, and at épée some use it as a semicircular parry in place of the 7th and 8th. At sabre it is one of the two low parries, made with the forearm pronated and a semicircular movement from the elbow. See [[Octave]], [[Prime]] and [[Lateral, Circular and Semicircular Parry]].

## Tierce
short: The third position: a high parry on the outside, with the hand pronated. A main parry at sabre.
source: S. Jefferson, Fencing: A Beginner's Guide, Volume 1, Herdwick Fencing Club (undated), glossary, p. 38; L. Rondelle, Foil and Sabre: A Grammar of Fencing (1892), pp. 19, 22 and 202; G. Heintz, Theory of Fencing with the Foil in Form of a Catechism (1890), p. 5; D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), sabre chapter, PDF pp. 82 to 84
status: library

Jefferson's glossary calls tierce a high line, pronated guard on the sword-arm side. Rondelle forms it by turning the nails down and carrying the hand slightly right until the forte meets the opponent's foible, the point staying level with the opponent's eye. He also uses tierce as another name for the sixte line, and Heintz calls the outer high opening the tierce opening. At [[Sabre]], Tyshler counts the 3rd, 4th and 5th as the three main upper parries. In close distance the elbow can go behind the trunk in the 3rd, which also guards the sword arm against cuts to the outside. Rondelle says tierce is the only useful sabre engagement between right-handers. See [[Sixte]], [[Engagement]] and [[Parry Positions]].

## Quarte
short: The fourth position: a high parry on the inside. The parry used most at foil.
source: S. Jefferson, Fencing: A Beginner's Guide, Volume 1, Herdwick Fencing Club (undated), glossary, p. 37; L. Rondelle, Foil and Sabre: A Grammar of Fencing (1892), pp. 22 to 23; D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), foil chapter, PDF pp. 53 to 54; IWAS Wheelchair Fencing Coaching Video 2: Four Simple Actions, Baldip Sahota and Jacques Portal, 00:04 to 00:18, 01:36 to 02:15 and 04:20
status: library

Jefferson's glossary calls quarte a high line, semi-supinated guard on the non-sword-arm side. Rondelle carries the hand left until the forte meets the opponent's foible, the point level with the opponent's left eye. Tyshler writes that the 4th takes the largest share of parries at foil, because it is the most varied in technique. Even when it fails, the armed arm and weapon cover much of the target, and a hit to the outside is harder because the blade must avoid the defender's arm. It is also the one parry where the elbow moves well back. The second of the WPF [[Four Simple Actions]] is the quarte parry and [[Riposte]], with the coach offering his blade at about 40 degrees to a beginner (see [[Presenting the Blade]]).

> My quarte was huge and the blade went way off target.
> Take it much tighter.

## Quinte
short: The fifth position: a low inside parry at foil and épée, but the head parry at sabre.
source: S. Jefferson, Fencing: A Beginner's Guide, Volume 1, Herdwick Fencing Club (undated), glossary, p. 37; L. Rondelle, Foil and Sabre: A Grammar of Fencing (1892), pp. 23 and 205; G. Heintz, Theory of Fencing with the Foil in Form of a Catechism (1890), pp. 5 to 7; D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), sabre chapter, PDF pp. 82 to 84
status: library

Jefferson's glossary gives two meanings: a low line, pronated guard on the non-sword-arm side at [[Foil]] and [[Épée]], and a high guard at [[Sabre]] to protect the head. Rondelle's foil quinte meets a thrust in low quarte: nails down, the blade forms a cross with the opponent's and drives it downward. He calls it the only parry that leaves the point out of line. Heintz presses the blade down to the left. Rondelle's sabre chapter names parries by what they protect, and his head parry raises the sabre level above the head. Tyshler counts the 5th among the three main upper sabre parries, against cuts from above. Its height may change with the attack, but not the direction of the point. See [[Parry Positions]] and [[Septime]].

## Sixte
short: The sixth position: a high parry on the outside, with the hand semi-supinated.
source: S. Jefferson, Fencing: A Beginner's Guide, Volume 1, Herdwick Fencing Club (undated), glossary, p. 38; L. Rondelle, Foil and Sabre: A Grammar of Fencing (1892), pp. 19 and 23; D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), foil and épée chapters, PDF pp. 45, 53, 62 and 69
status: library

Jefferson's glossary calls sixte a high line, semi-supinated guard on the sword-arm side. Rondelle carries the hand right with the thumb up, keeping the point level with the opponent's eye. His outside line is the sixte line, also called tierce: both parries guard the same area with the hand turned differently (see [[Tierce]]). Tyshler names the 4th and 6th as the main upper parries at foil and épée, and the circular 6th as the circular parry used most at épée. His foil chapter shows the choice it leaves: from the 6th position, a hit inside can be met by a direct 4th, a circular 6th, a semicircular 2nd or 8th, or a high 7th. Tyshler's five sabre parries do not include it. See [[Quarte]] and [[Counter-Parry]].

## Septime
short: The seventh position: a low parry on the inside, with the hand semi-supinated.
source: S. Jefferson, Fencing: A Beginner's Guide, Volume 1, Herdwick Fencing Club (undated), glossary, pp. 37 to 38; L. Rondelle, Foil and Sabre: A Grammar of Fencing (1892), pp. 21 and 24; G. Heintz, Theory of Fencing with the Foil in Form of a Catechism (1890), pp. 5 to 7; D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), foil and épée chapters, PDF pp. 47, 53 and 69
status: library

Jefferson's glossary calls septime a low line, semi-supinated guard on the non-sword-arm side. Rondelle lists it as septime, or half-circle: against an attack in low quarte, the point drops in a half circle under the opponent's wrist and the hand moves a little left at the same height. Heintz calls the same parry lower quarte. Tyshler names the 7th and 8th as the main low parries at foil and épée. His foil list pairs the 7th with a direct riposte, or a high 7th with a riposte by [[Coupé]] to the back. In wheelchair fencing every target ends at the hips (see [[Target Area]]), so a low parry guards only the lower trunk. See [[Octave]] and [[Parry Positions]].

## Octave
short: The eighth position: a low parry on the outside, with the hand semi-supinated.
source: S. Jefferson, Fencing: A Beginner's Guide, Volume 1, Herdwick Fencing Club (undated), glossary, p. 36; L. Rondelle, Foil and Sabre: A Grammar of Fencing (1892), p. 24; G. Heintz, Theory of Fencing with the Foil in Form of a Catechism (1890), pp. 5 to 7; R. Howard, Essentials of Fencing Technique, in Key Skills in Blade Fencing (undated), PDF p. 14; D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), foil and épée chapters, PDF pp. 47, 69 and 70
status: library

Jefferson's glossary calls octave a low line, semi-supinated guard on the sword-arm side. Rondelle forms it against a thrust in low sixte: the point drops in a half circle under the opponent's wrist, the thumb turned slightly right, and the hand moves a little right at the same height. Heintz calls it lower tierce. Howard says the [[Croisé]] has its most control when taken from sixte down to octave. In Tyshler's foil list the 8th is the low parry used most, with a riposte to the upper sector, and the 2nd can replace it. At épée he shows a riposte from the 4th parry with an envelopment into the 8th engagement and a hit to the leg, which is not target in the wheelchair. See [[Seconde]], [[Septime]] and [[Target Area]].

## Counter-Parry
short: A circular parry: the point makes a full circle round the attacking blade and brings it back to the line it started from.
source: L. Rondelle, Foil and Sabre: A Grammar of Fencing (1892), pp. 21 to 24; G. Heintz, Theory of Fencing with the Foil in Form of a Catechism (1890), pp. 7 to 8; S. Jefferson, Fencing: A Beginner's Guide, Volume 1, Herdwick Fencing Club (undated), glossary, pp. 33 to 34; D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), chapter Terminology, PDF p. 41; IWAS Wheelchair Fencing Coaching Video 2: Four Simple Actions, Baldip Sahota and Jacques Portal, 02:15 to 02:47
status: library

Rondelle calls it the counter: a small but complete circle of the point, with the hand still, that finds the attacking blade wherever it threatens and carries it to the opposite line. He gives a counter for each of the eight parries. Heintz describes following the opponent's blade with a steady hand back to the side it started from. Jefferson's glossary sends counter-parry to circular parry. Take care with the Russian term: Tyshler's kontrzashchita is the parry of the opponent's riposte, not a circular parry. The WPF video uses counter parry in that sense too: the coach parries and ripostes, and the seated fencer takes the counter parry and hits again. See [[Lateral, Circular and Semicircular Parry]] and [[Counter-Riposte]].

## Lateral, Circular and Semicircular Parry
short: The three paths a parry can take: straight across, in a half circle between high and low, or in a full circle.
source: D. A. Tyshler (ed.), Sportivnoe fekhtovanie [Sport Fencing] (1997), chapter Terminology, PDF pp. 38 to 39 and 42, and foil chapter, PDF p. 54; R. Howard, Essentials of Fencing Technique, in Key Skills in Blade Fencing (undated), PDF p. 6; S. Jefferson, Fencing: A Beginner's Guide, Volume 1, Herdwick Fencing Club (undated), glossary, pp. 33, 34 and 37
status: library

Tyshler sorts parries, like beats and takings, by the path of the blade: direct, in the horizontal plane; semicircular, in the vertical plane; and circular, moving in both and returning to the starting position. Howard calls the first kind lateral, as from sixte to quarte. His semicircular parry traces an arc with the point from one line to another, and his counterparry a full circle. Jefferson's glossary has the semicircular parry go from high to low on the same side, and adds a diagonal parry from a high guard to the low guard on the opposite side. Tyshler's foil chapter notes that a [[General Parry]] usually joins two of these paths. See [[Counter-Parry]], [[Parry Positions]] and [[Parry]].

## Direct and Indirect Riposte
short: A riposte that goes straight to the target in the line of the parry, or one that first passes under or over the opponent's blade.
source: S. Jefferson, Fencing: A Beginner's Guide, Volume 1, Herdwick Fencing Club (undated), glossary, pp. 34 and 36; R. Howard, Essentials of Fencing Technique, in Key Skills in Blade Fencing (undated), PDF p. 6; L. Rondelle, Foil and Sabre: A Grammar of Fencing (1892), pp. 27 to 28; IWAS Wheelchair Fencing Coaching Video 2: Four Simple Actions, Baldip Sahota and Jacques Portal, 02:01 to 02:15
status: library

Jefferson's glossary calls an action direct when it is made without passing the blade under or over the opponent's, and indirect when it first passes it. Howard's examples are the riposte by [[Glide]], which is direct, and the riposte by [[Coupé]], which is indirect. Rondelle divides the direct [[Riposte]] by timing. The tac-au-tac is made at once, in the line of the parry, as the opponent's front foot lands. The riposte in opposition comes after making sure of one's safety, against a blade that stays close. In the WPF video, after the parry the coach checks the riposte for the straight arm, the guard above the shoulder and the angle of the body. See [[Disengage]] and [[Four Simple Actions]].
